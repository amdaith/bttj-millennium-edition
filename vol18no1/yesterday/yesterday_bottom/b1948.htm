The Philosophy of PCM

Proceedings of the IRE, Vol.36, pp1324-1332, Nov 1948

B M Oliver, J R Pierce and Claude E Shannon

Synopsis

Although the invention of Pulse Code Modulation (PCM) was the result of the work of Alec Reeves in 1937, this paper did much to explain, in relatively simple terms, the key advantages of PCM and resulted in a popular understanding of the subject.

The paper is important since it is the work of three gifted expositors and leaders in their field. It brings out the deciding factors that laid the foundations for the ultimate conversion of the telecommunications network to create a 'digital world'.

A Mathmatical Theory of Communication

Bell System Technical Journal, Vol.27, No.3, pp379-424, July 1948

Claude E Shannon

Synopsis

Claude Shannon is the creator of the science of information theory. He was born in 1916 in Michigan from where he graduated from the University of Michigan before completing his Doctorate from MIT. He joined Bell Telephone Laboratories in 1941 as a research mathematician and in 1948 published his monumental and definitive paper "A mathematical theory of communication" which has served as the definitive treatment of the subject for communications engineers ever since. His earlier work was concerned with the relationship of Boolean algebra and switching machines and is the subject of another seminal contribution to the science of communications.

While communication theory is a general theory with application to all forms of communications, its principles provide important guidance in efficient coding algorithms by putting these principles into action. Indeed, as Shannon points out in this paper, the transducer which does the encoding should match the source of the channel in a statistical sense in an analogous way to that in which a transformer is used to maximise the transfer of power from a source to a load.

The vitality of Shannon's work is increasingly recognised as his theory is applied to other fields of study such as linguistics, biology and many others.

The Transistor, A Semiconductor Triode

Physical Review , Vol.74, No.2, pp230-231, Nov 1948

J Bardeen and W H Brattain

Synopsis

There had been a number of reports from the 1920s onwards of oscillations in crystal diodes and one or two describing effects involving the use of a third electrode. No sound theoretical understanding existed to support such claims however. It was the work of the three Nobel laureates John Bardeen, Walter Brattain and William Shockley, all of Bell Telephone Laboratories, that produced the invention of the transistor, as we know it today. No other invention in the 20th century has so profoundly affected the rate of progress that has been seen in the field of telecommunications and computers.

The invention of the transistor, a name invented by John Pierce of Bell Labs, required, in his words "both ideas and things". The ideas were the theoretical understanding of semiconductors which only quantum mechanics could provide. The things were extremely pure semiconducting material in which free electrons and positive charges could exist together for appreciable lengths of time. The third ingredient for the invention of the transistor was the application of able and enquiring minds to grapple with the dual problem of making a new type of amplifier and understanding the complex and puzzling phenomena encountered. This was amply provided by the genius of its inventors.

This early announcement paper describes the new electronic device consisting of a block of germanium with two point electrodes, together with a low resistance base electrode. The paper reports that power gains of up to 20 db had been observed and this was attributed to the existence of a thin surface film layer which was a 'deficit semi-conductor', i.e. different from the bulk of the material.
