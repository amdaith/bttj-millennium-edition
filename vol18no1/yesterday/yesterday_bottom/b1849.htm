Experimental Researches in Electricity

Taylor and Francis, London. 1839 - 1855. (Three Volumes)

Michael Faraday

Synopsis

Faraday, who was born in Surrey, England in 1791, received little more than an elementary formal education but through a fortuitous set of circumstances became assistant to Sir Humphry Davy from whom he learnt much of science. Although he knew little mathematics, he was intrigued by the interaction of electric charges and currents. In searching for some graphic way of representing the nature of the forces he found, he hit upon the idea of electric and magnetic forces. Although Faraday's ideas of curved fields in the space outside the objects were not accepted by many of his contemporaries, there was one exception, James Clerk Maxwell, who accepted Faraday's fundamental field theory and later expressed it mathematically in those wonderful equations which have been the bedrock of understanding electromagnetic theory ever since.

Although Faraday was responsible for the discovery of benzene, it was in electricity and magnetism that he made his major discoveries. Not the least of which was the discovery of electromagnetism. He was attracted to this field of study by the work of Oersted on compass needle experiments and in 1821 produced a simple electric motor. It was his discovery 10 years later that must rate as his greatest, however, with the experiments on magnetic induction which allowed him to state the fundamental rules of electromagnetic induction.

Although his researches were published in the scientific literature of the day, it was his monumental work 'Experimental Researches' which he published at the age of 64 that summarised much of his life's work. He died in London in 1867.
