Researches in Telephony

Proceedings of the American Academy of Arts and Sciences, Vol.XII, Boston, 1877

Alexander Graham Bell

Synopsis

Alexander Graham Bell was born in Edinburgh, Scotland in 1847 and emigrated to North America with his parents, first to Canada and then to the USA. Although Bell devoted himself to researches into deafness, he is of course known for his invention of the telephone. Surely, no other invention transformed the way of life in Bell's 75-year lifetime. At the time of his birth railways and telegraphy were only beginning to be established but at the time of his death, telephonic communication was not only well established but was already having its impact on the very structure of society.

Two years before he emigrated, Bell was drawn to the work of Hermann von Helmholtz, which led him to the study of sound waves. Bell filed his first patent on the telephone in March 1876 and his second (which employed a permanent magnet, thus eliminating the need for a battery) in January 1887. The invention brought with it much litigation but Bell's priority was upheld. Thomas Edison, however, is credited with the invention of the variable resistance carbon microphone which, until recently, was almost universally used in all telephone handsets due to its higher amplitude signal. The Bell invention relied on technology that had been in existence for some time. Specifically, from before Bell was born, Oersted in 1820 discovered electromagnetism, Faraday had discovered electromagnetic induction in 1831 and in 1837 Wheatstone had invented the electric telegraph. It was the idea of transmitting sound-representing waves that was the unique aspect of Bell's work, which captured the essence of the invention of the telephone.

Alexander Graham Bell was also visionary. His prophetic words to a group of businessmen in the summer of 1877, were highly significant: "I believe, in the future, wires will unite the head offices of the Telephone Company in different cities, and a man in one part of the country may communicate by word of mouth with another in a distant place". Continuing, "I will impress upon you all the advisability of keeping this end in view, that all present arrangements of the telephone may be eventually realised in this grand system". It should be recognised that this statement was made just two years from the time of the first electrical transmission of the vibrations of a metal reed.

Throughout his lifetime's work, Bell maintained the dual activities of his tuned system of multiple telegraphy and the study of voice sound induced air waves in the ear. His claims to fame go beyond the founding of the American telephone industry, and these were recognised in his many awards including the Hughes Medal of the Royal Society.

Although born in Scotland, and for almost four decades spending the greater part of his year in Nova Scotia, upon his death in 1922, he had his tombstone record that he was an American citizen.
