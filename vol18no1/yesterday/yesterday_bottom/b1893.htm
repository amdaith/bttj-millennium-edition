Electromagnetic Theory

Earnest Benn Ltd. London, 1893

O Heaviside

Synopsis

Oliver Heaviside was born in London in 1850 and died in Devon, England in 1925. He was a founder of the theory of cable telegraphy and also noted for his discovery of the upper reflecting layer in the atmosphere, which bears his name. Heaviside was to a large extent self-taught, having not received a university education. His career started as a telegraph operator with the Great Northern Telegraph Company at Newcastle upon Tyne but he was forced to retire due to deafness. He then lived with his parents from 1876 until they died and then moved to live with his brother in Devon where he spent the rest of his life.

Despite his lack of formal education, Heaviside's private and solitary study led him to master much mathematical and physical theory. His approach to the subjects was at times unconventional, which undoubtedly led to his work not being fully appreciated in the early years. Indeed, against criticism of fellow workers' difficulties with his published work, he wrote "Fault has been found with these articles that they are hard to read. They were harder perhaps to write!" His contributions to Telegraph Theory had significance beyond even their immediate technical importance. His publications are extensive and are largely contained in his two-volume set of 'Electrical Papers' and his three volume set 'Electromagnetic Theory'. In his studies of cable propagation he added his own new concept of inductance to the theory of Lord Kelvin and was able to show how waves could be propagated without distortion (although with attenuation). This he expressed in his introduction to the above work as follows:

"Now, I was so fortunate as to discover, during the examination of a practical telephonic problem, that a certain case of propagation along a conducting circuit through a conducting dielectric, the transcendality of the mathematics automatically vanished, by the distorting effects on an electromagnetic wave, of the resistance of the conductor, and the conductance of the dielectric, being of opposite natures, so that they neutralised one another, and rendered the circuit non-distortional or distortionless. The mathematics was, in the main, reduced to simple algebra, and the manner of transmission of disturbances could be examined in complete detail in an elementary manner".

Heaviside's understanding of electric conduction owed much to the electromagnetic theory of Maxwell and he has been viewed as a bridge between the work of Maxwell and Hertz, work he anticipated in some respects. His work on distortionless lines led to the work of Pupil on loading coils in cables and later to the work of George Campbell.

In his later life, his solitary and isolated existence, induced to some extent by his deafness, led to some degree of eccentricity and his work attracted little attention. It was Lord Kelvin's reference to him in his Presidential address to the Institution of Electrical Engineers in 1889 which seems to have brought him to greater recognition. In fact, he was elected FRS two years later and in 1908 he was elected Honorary Member of the Institution of Electrical Engineers (having previously been struck from their membership rolls for his inability to pay his annual subscription). He died in relative poverty in Devon in 1925.

Electric Waves

Macmillan and Co., London 1893

H R Hertz

Synopsis

Heinrich Hertz was born in Hamburg, Germany in 1857 and died in Bonn at the early age of 37 in 1894. He made major contributions to radio science and has been described as the father of radio communication. His major contributions stem from one of the major scientific problems and, at the time, controversies in the nineteenth century. The question revolved around whether forces acted at a distance regardless of an intervening medium or whether the latter plays an essential role. The general division of opinion was that continental physicists favoured the former view while the English school favoured the latter. In fact, Maxwell in his classic work had shown that all known electric and magnetic phenomena could be interpreted in terms of stresses and motions in the material medium. In fact, this proved to be so but in spite of this and much evidence to support it, many of Hertz's contemporaries had difficulty accepting this until he was able to present his direct experimental verification of this. As Hertz puts it in the introduction to his work:

"I have often been asked how I was first led to carry out the experiments which are described in the following pages. The general inducement was this. In the year 1879 the Berlin Academy of Science had offered a prize for research on the following problem: - To establish experimentally any relation between electromagnetic forces and the dielectric polarisation of insulators - that is to say, either an electromagnetic force exerted by polarisation in non-conductors, or the polarisation of a non-conductor as an effect of electromagnetic induction. As I was at that time engaged upon electromagnetic researches at the Physical Institute in Berlin, Herr von Helmholtz drew my attention to this problem, and promised that I should have the assistance of the Institute in case I decided to take up the work. I reflected on this problem, and considered what results might be expected under favourable conditions by using the oscillations of Leyden Jars or of open induction-coils. The conclusion at which I arrived was decidedly not what I wished for; it appeared that any decided effect could scarcely be hoped for, but only an action lying just within the limits of observation. I therefore gave up the idea of working at the problem; nor am I aware that it has been attacked by anybody else. But in spite of having abandoned the solution at that time, I still felt ambitious to discover it by some other method; and my interest in everything connected with electric oscillations had become keener. It was scarcely possible that I should overlook any new form of such oscillations, in any case a happy chance should bring such within my notice. Such a chance occurred to me in the spring of 1886 ………."

In Hertz's first important experiment, he used a piece of wire in the form of a rectangle with a gap. When this was connected to a circuit through which an induction coil was discharged, a spark jumped the gap. Hertz found the spark could be induced even without connection to the oscillating circuit, provided the dimensions of the two circuits were substantially the same. These experiments allowed him to detect electric waves at some 60 feet from the source. He also found that such waves (of 24 cm) behaved in a similar way to those of light. In his own words, "Certainly it is a fascinating idea that the processes in air which we have been investigating represent to us on a million-fold larger scale the same processes which go on in the neighbourhood of a Fresnel mirror or between the glass plates used for exhibiting Newton's rings"

Hertz's results were of tremendous scientific significance and laid the foundations for the work of Marconi who, inspired by reports of this work, was able to span the Atlantic with Hertzian waves in 1901.
