Certain Topics in Telegraph Transmission

AIEE, Vol.47 pp214-216

H Nyquist

Synopsis

Harry Nyquist is probably best known for his ground-breaking work on establishing stability criteria in feedback amplifiers, which had been invented by Harold Black. His contribution to the field of communications sciences are many, however, and at the time of his retirement from Bell Laboratories in 1954 he had attributed to him almost 150 patents. His contributions to the expressions for the thermal noise effect are significant in his work.

A major contribution of Nyquist's was his early theoretical work on determining the bandwidth requirements for transmitting various time domain communication signals. This work laid the early foundations for later advances by Claude Shannon, which led to the development of Information Theory, as we now know it.

This paper deals with the question of the nature of the distortion on telegraph signals but also covers the basic principles of later application in pulse code modulation systems. Although the paper specifically deals with the topic from the standpoint of baseband steady state considerations, it also addresses the implications of double and single sideband (as invented by John Carson) transmission systems.

The Transmission of Information

Bell System Technical Journal Vol.7, pp535-564, July 1928

R V L Hartley

Synopsis

Hartley was a graduate of the University of Utah and a Rhodes scholar and was first known for his disclosures in 1915 of a class of thermionic valve oscillator circuits, which became known as the Hartley oscillator. This work was completed while Hartley was with Western Electric's Engineering Department. His latter contributions were concerned with the bandwidth of a transmission circuit and its ability to transmit 'information'.

Hartley published his results in the late twenties following on from the mathematical studies of Nyquist. The results were to be the guiding rules for transmission engineers for two decades. The analyses were primarily directed to noiseless channels and it was the treatment of channel noise by Shannon in the late 1940s, which presented a more complete coverage of the problem.

This paper introduces a quantitative measure of 'information' and the concept is developed based on physical considerations. The limitations on the transmission of information over a system are considered and the relation between the steady state and the transient viewpoints is considered. The important relationship, which states that the amount of information that can be transmitted is proportional to the amount of bandwidth and the time of its availability, is developed and applied to practical systems.

Radio Communication

Proceedings of the IRE Vol.16, pp40-69, 1928 (Reprinted in Proceedings IEEE Oct 1998)

Senatore Guglielmo Marconi

Synopsis

Guglielmo Marconi was born in Bologna, Italy in 1874. He was educated by private tutors and at the Technical Institute in Leghorn. In the year that Hertz died, in 1894, he came across an account of the possibilities of Hertzian waves in a technical publication. This was the stimulus for a series of experiments, from which, within twelve months, he established wireless communication over a distance of more than a mile. In 1896 Marconi took out, in the United Kingdom, the first patent for wireless telegraphy. Various demonstrations were made and in 1897 Marconi established a Wireless Telegraphy Company in London and in 1901 the first transatlantic demonstration of the transmission of radio signals was made. He was awarded the Nobel Prize in 1909. His remarkable position in the history of radio was due to his ability to combine experimental skills with the commercial skill for the exploitation of his results.

The current paper gives an historic sketch of Marconi's investigations on short waves and the application of these to radio communications over long distances. The paper refers to the value of 'reflectors' and observes zones of weak and variable reception, which coincide approximately with 'skip-distances'.

Thermal Agitation of Electricity in Conductors

Physical Review Vol.32, pp97-109, July 1928

J B Johnson

Synopsis

John Bertrand Johnson, in penning a short biography of himself, succinctly described one of his activities as "cleared up fundamental sources of circuit noise in 1925 - 30". In a similar vein he described his achievement of producing the world's first sealed cathode commercial CRT as "made C R oscilloscope a common laboratory tool in 1922".

It was for his work at Bell Telephone Laboratories on analysing the most ubiquitous form of circuit noise, that due to thermal agitation, that he is best known and which now bears his name. Johnson noise is the low frequency portion of the electromagnetic radiation sent out by all hot bodies. It was the spectrum of radiation associated with hot bodies that Max Planck explained satisfactorily in 1900, in the first step towards our understanding of quantum phenomena.

Johnson's work on vacuum-tube amplifiers allowed him to demonstrate the existence of this noise and to further predict the maximum degree of useful amplification that could be expected due to this source of noise. His work has been universally applied since and the phenomenon is known as the Johnson effect.

In this paper, starting with a consideration of the fluctuating charge in all conductors, Johnson goes on to an analysis which relates the magnitude of the resultant noise in terms of fundamental constants and so predict ultimate useful limits on amplification in vacuum-tube amplifiers.

Thermal Agitation of Electric Charge in Conductors

Physical Review Vol.32, pp110-114, July 1928

H Nyquist

Synopsis

Harry Nyquist (see previous entry) has been described as having had an almost uncanny ability to solve a problem and produce an almost simple minded scheme that was far better than other approaches. So it was with his work on Johnson noise. When Johnson discovered the thermal noise effect he discussed it with Nyquist. As Johnson wrote some time later "he came up with the famous formula for the effect, based effectively on the thermodynamics of a telephone line and covering almost every detail that one needs to know about thermal noise".

The Nyquist formula is the now well known:

Mean Square Noise Voltage = 4kTRW

where k is Boltzmann's constant, T is absolute temperature, R is the resistance and W is the bandwidth

This paper derives this quantification based on the principles of thermodynamics and statistical mechanics and agrees with the observed results of Johnson
