Self-Organising Data Networks

Background

The concepts of replication and caching have been exploited for years in the domains of computer hardware and operating systems. With its tremendous growth, together with strong locality of reference observed in its wide-area data access patterns, the Internet seems to be quite suitable for expanding the above concepts into the network itself. Network caching and replication involve the storing of multiple copies of data objects in distributed locations throughout the network. Access to data is provided by these nearby copies, saving the need to go all the way to the original source. The results are:

  • reduced user perceived latency,
  • reduced bandwidth consumption on the backbone network,
  • load reduction on the origin server,
  • improved data availability through redundancy,
  • possibility of providing Quality of service (QoS) guarantees to the end user.

There have been a number of initiatives mainly from the academic community to promote the wide deployment of Internet caching [1]. Until recently, due to lack of commercial interest this deployment has been somewhat slower than expected. But this is likely to change as large Internet Service Providers (ISPs) are realising the benefits of the caching solution. This realisation has been significantly helped and influenced by results from simulation models clearly indicating the performance and business benefits of caching and replication [2, 3, 4, 5].

To understand the rationale behind the decision to invest in cache networks, it is better to think in terms of Content Providers (CPs), ISPs and end users (figure 1).

Fig 1. Schematic representation of Internet users that access Web pages provided by Content Providers (CPs) through an Internet Service Provider (ISP). The ISP is using a cache to store locally copies of previously accessed Web pages.

 

The ISP is interested in building a cache to improve user perceived latency and reduce bandwidth consumption to the Internet backbone (for which the ISP is being charged). Latency measure is important for the ISP because this is what the end user is interested in. The faster the end user downloads requested Web pages, the better the perceived service and the less he pays for his Internet access assuming the current UK pricing model in which the user only pays the charge of a metered local telephone call. Reduction in used bandwidth alone is not sufficient by itself to financially justify building the cache [3]. However, it is beneficial to the ISP since more bandwidth will be made available for other applications, especially those requiring real-time streaming.

The CP on the other hand is interested in replicating all its data to ISPs, thus providing the user with better service and even being able to guarantee some QoS. To achieve specified QoS it is likely that all files must be replicated, even those that are rarely accessed. The main difference between replication and caching is therefore the following. Replication copies on to one site the whole set of files without any dynamic consideration of request distribution, whereas caching is based on demand-driven adaptation mechanism where the least recently used replacement algorithms guarantee that only recently used files are cached locally. Less popular files may have to be got from the original site.

It is interesting to note that in many cases the CPs do not like caching since it moves the control of the data from the CP to the ISP. This is particularly true in cases where the CPs provide marketing and advertising. Consider, for example, a scenario in which the revenues collected by a CP correspond to the number of hits to the pages containing the advertisements. If the CP's pages are cached, no hits will be registered at the CP's server when the cached pages are visited and thus no revenues will be collected. This is one of the reasons why some CPs explicitly mark their pages as 'non-cachable'.
