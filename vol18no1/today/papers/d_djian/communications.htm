Towards Human-Centred Intelligent Systems: The Intelligent Assistant

Communication Management

In a modern office environment there are two main issues in managing communication:

  1. to provide support to initiate outgoing communications,
  2. to handle incoming interruptions.

Here we focus on (2), which usually affects the concentration of a user and reduces their productivity.

Telephone and e-mail assistants

In this context, managing interruptions can mean preventing the phone from ringing for an incoming call, or giving various priorities to incoming e-mail messages. The IA uses two smart assistants: the telephone assistant (TA) and the e-mail assistant (EA).

The main difficulty in defining a reasoning process for these assistants is that, as conditions surrounding an interruption vary, so does the required behaviour of the assistants. This is why they must build a context around each interruption. Moreover, this reasoning mechanism must be:

  • flexible in order to take into account the uncertainty in the knowledge the systems has of the real world, i.e. this call is likely to be urgent, rather than this call is urgent,

  • adaptable to a change of requirements over time because users' priorities change, for instance when a new project starts or when an old one finishes.

The telephone and e-mail assistants have tackled these issues with Bayes nets (see example in figure 4). Bayes nets (or causal nets) are useful to represent uncertain domain knowledge with prior probabilities. They can compute posterior probabilities based on pieces of evidence observed by the assistants. Finally, they can adapt these probabilities through experience to account for changes in the environment.

Context of an interruption

In the IA, defining the context of an interruption uses three levels of information:

  • information local to the interruption, e.g. who is calling,
  • information local to the assistant, e.g. call log in the telephone assistant,
  • information global to the IA, i.e. information from another assistant such as the diary assistant.

The Bayes net shown in figure 4 is used by the telephone assistant to compute a priority for each incoming phone call. Nodes correspond to discrete random variables, and directed links represent relationships quantified by conditional probabilities P(node | parent). For instance, node DiarySoon holds the probability that there is, in the near future, a diary entry related to the interruption (e.g. a meeting with the caller) given that the interruption is Urgent (prior knowledge or knowledge learnt from examples). Note the distinction between features relating to urgency and features relating to importance.

Adaptability

The key here is to adapt to the user's preferences. For this reason, the telephone and e-mail assistants learn by watching the user's actions. The telephone assistant looks at whether or not the user answers an incoming phone call to increase or reduce the priority associated with the caller. The e-mail assistant can use more information associated with an incoming message to refine the priority model. This information is provided by the header fields of an e-mail message such as To, From and Subject. In this case, the e-mail assistant learns by watching the level of priority that the user assigns to an incoming message. Decreasing priority levels are represented by read now, today, this week, this month or never (figure 6).
