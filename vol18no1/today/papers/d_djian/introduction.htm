Towards Human-Centred Intelligent Systems: The Intelligent Assistant

Introduction

Human-centred intelligent systems

Early attempts at building systems with comparable cognitive capability to the human brain have not been as successful as predicted. The problems of knowledge representation, commonsense knowledge and reasoning, real-time problem solving, vision, speech, and language processing have not as yet yielded to classical AI techniques. Despite the difficulties, AI has been successfully applied in some areas such as expert systems, classification, planning, scheduling, game playing and robotics. The focus is on systems that can augment the cognitive capabilities of humans, i.e. a human-machine symbiosis or Human-Centred Intelligent System (HCIS) [1]. HCIS, are different in many respects from traditional AI systems in that they do not compete with, or try to match, human level intelligence. Rather, they assist humans in performing cognitive tasks such that the combination of the human and the system has a greater cognitive capacity than each on its own.

Soft Computing techniques

One of many promising approaches for developing hybrid intelligent systems is Soft Computing (SC) [2]. The term soft computing was coined by Lotfi Zadeh [6], the inventor of fuzzy set theory [5]. Zadeh defines SC as a partnership between fuzzy logic (FL), neuro-computing (NC), probabilistic reasoning (PR), with the latter subsuming genetic algorithms (GA), chaotic systems, belief networks and parts of learning theory [7].

The philosophical argument for SC is stated elegantly by Mamdani [4]. It is obvious that humans deal with uncertain and imprecise information every day and are remarkably consistent in processing such information. This is the primary aim of SC, to exploit the tolerance of imprecision, uncertainty and 'partial truth' associated with almost every aspect of real-world problems.

Real-world problems are typically ill defined, difficult to model and with large-scale solution spaces [3]. In these cases precise models are impractical, too expensive or non-existent. Therefore there is a need for approximate modelling techniques capable of handling imperfect and sometimes conflicting information. Soft Computing offers a framework for integrating such techniques (figure 1).

The Intelligent Assistant (IA)

The term personal assistant or agent is used to describe a software system that performs a task on behalf of its user (figure 2).
 

Fig 2. The intelligent assistant (IA)

The main functions of the IA can be summarised as prioritising tasks, handling requests, developing user profiles and communicating with the user. The IA consists of a number of digital assistants specialising in specific tasks as shown in figure 3. Soft Computing techniques provide these assistants with the flexibility required to adapt to and interact with the user.
 

Fig 3. Components of the intelligent assistant

The IA provides assistance in five specific types of tasks.

  • Communication management

The IA is capable of handling e-mails and telephone calls and learns how the user performs such tasks in order to mimic the user's responses. User feedback is an essential part of the learning process. Bayes nets can handle both the learning aspect and the uncertainty associated with the predicted priority of incoming messages and phone calls.

  • Information management

The IA can develop the knowledge necessary to proactively gather information relating to the user's tasks, activities and interests. Learning forms a major part of this activity, i.e. the IA will need to evolve over time with their users as interests and activities change. Soft Computing can help the IA to refine or expand vague user queries.

  • Time management

The IA uses AI techniques to dynamically schedule user tasks based on given user preferences and observations of the user's previous actions, work habits and interactions. Fuzzy scheduling is well adapted to handle vague times (e.g. in the morning) and durations (e.g. around 1 hour).

  • User interaction management

The IA can interact with the user through multiple channels of communication. Fuzzy temporal reasoning helps with interpreting user requests, and artificial neural networks are central to a gaze tracker.
