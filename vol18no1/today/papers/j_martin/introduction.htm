Using Genetics To Extract Information

Introduction

We may very well know what information we are interested in, but unfortunately, computers are not adaptable enough to understand us, yet! This is why, when faced with the task of interfacing with a database, we need to elaborate a query that contains all the bits of information, e.g. keywords, that the machine needs. Now, suppose that we took all these bits together and linked them with an enquiring glue (Boolean functions). If we could build lots of different queries randomly, there would be a chance that one query would perform better than any of ours; generating and analysing such a number of queries is not a problem for computers.

We could also let the computer evaluate all the keyword-based queries depending on how well they do at retrieving a set of documents that we have previously marked as interesting (the training set) and reward these by increasing the probability of being selected to replicate in a life-like situation where only the fittest survive. If, in addition, we allow for genetic recombination and mutation we ensure the diversity needed to explore the solution space; this approach has been proved to work by Nature itself.

BTGP is an example of what we know as Genetic Programming (GP) where the individuals or creatures are computer programs in the form of a tree. GP is an extended application of an earlier field known as Genetic Algorithms (GA) [1]. Both are capable of solving optimisation and design problems, as well as computer methods for learning speech, artificial vision, financial trading and many other applications. GP and GA are characterised for their robustness and adaptability in providing solutions to these problems. For a discussion of GP and its relationship with other evolutionary algorithms, see Kinnear [2].

The original GP was written by John Koza [3, 4] in LISP. There are also some non-commercial versions in C and C++ in the existing literature [5, 6, 7] and good guides about the implementation issues [8, 9].
