Fare Well, My Clone!

Ever since the furore over Dolly the Sheep, any talk about clones has tended to give me an uncontrollable fit of yawning. The very last thing I ever imagined was that one day I should get personally involved in the subject - and in the most far-out way imaginable.

A few weeks ago I was approached by a slightly amazing character, name of Dr Rick Fleeter. He and his tiny company, AeroAstro (some twenty five employees!) have built nineteen satellites, which are now performing all sorts of useful tasks - astronomical and geophysical research, global surveying, space communications, etc...

The most amazing thing about AeroAstro's satellites is that they are tiny. I have one on my desk at the moment; it's smaller than a dinner plate, and weighs less than 2 pounds (one kilogram)! This means that they are also very cheap; few of Rick's products cost as much as a million dollars, and I suspect that his activities are regarded with considerable dismay by the dinosaurs of the aerospace industry, who are accustomed to hundred megabuck contracts.

Now, however, he is involved in a space project so novel that the big boys wouldn't touch it, because of the giggle factor. Rick is offering - though certainly not guaranteeing - a form of immortality, to perhaps a million customers.

Something remotely similar was suggested in a 1930s science-fiction story - Neil R Jones' 'The Jameson Satellite'. Escaping from the war-ravaged 26th Century, Professor Jameson had his body placed in an artificial satellite, which circled the Earth for millions of years until it was discovered - long after humanity was extinct - by a team of robot space explorers. They downloaded the professor's brain into a spare chassis, and he happily accompanied them on their interstellar adventures.

Well, Rick Fleeter's project is much more sophisticated, though it too would depend upon the intervention of alien technologists in the far future. Instead of launching a whole body into space, why not send a few DNA strands? Then - in principle at least - any human being could be re-created, physically if not mentally.

Despite this obvious limitation, AeroAstro's 'Encounter Project' does have much more appeal than the recent Celestis enterprise, in which small samples of deceased persons' ashes were sent into orbit, eventually burning up on re-entry. 'Star Trek's' creator, Gene Roddenberry, was one of the first customers for this double cremation. However, you do not have to die to sign up for Encounter; you merely have to contribute a few hairs.

It is indeed astonishing that every visit to the barber's leaves enough information on the floor to re-create more humans than have ever lived - a hundred billion, give or take a few million. When we will have the skill to grow a DNA sample into a live person is another question, but Dolly suggests that the answer will be around next Wednesday. To any super-civilisation out there it would be a trivial accomplishment, its origin lost in prehistory back with stone axes and TV sets.

So Rick Fleeter hopes to collect sufficient customers to pay the very modest cost of the mission - the weight involved would be only a few kilograms - for a launch in 2001 aboard the highly reliable Ariane rocket when it heads towards geostationary orbit. With its independent propulsion system, the payload would then be boosted towards Jupiter, utilising the 'slingshot' effect of the giant planet's gravitational field to escape from the Solar System. When its final trajectory had been determined, messages would be sent ahead of it from one of our radio telescopes, to alert anyone along its future orbit.

But here we have a serious problem of timing. Despite its boost from Jupiter, the Encounter probe will only be crawling starwards at perhaps one ten-thousandth of the speed of light - so it would take some fifty thousand years to reach even our nearest stellar neighbour! It is probably optimistic to suggest that there's any other civilisation within a million years, at such a pitiful cruising speed.

Yet even that is a short time on the cosmic scale, and the experiment might well be successful, though we would never know. As important as the duration of the mission is the survivability of DNA in the space environment. Obviously the payload would be well protected, and the risk of any physical collision is negli- gible: the real danger would be from cosmic radiation. However, even the smallest sample of tissue contains millions of identical DNA molecules, and they couldn't all be damaged in the same places along the double helix. It would be a fairly straight- forward task to reconstruct an uncorrupted version.

So let us assume that somewhere in the Galaxy, the Encounter payload is captured, and the creatures who open this ancient time-capsule have the technology, and the motivation, to take the experiment to its conclusion. This would result in a really bizarre situation - the genesis of parentless human beings with perfectly blank minds, in an inconceivably strange environment. Could they be educated, or even taught to speak, by custodians of a completely alien species, perhaps with utterly different sense organs? Or would they always remain mindless biological robots?

I do have some moral qualms when I try to imagine my clone coping with foster-parents who might look more grotesque than an octopus, a praying mantis, or T. Rex. Yet as he would never know anything different, he would probably take his new family for granted.

And perhaps they are the ones who would be surprised. Though it now requires sensitive instruments to detect what is left of my hair, I have always tried to keep the remnants in order by a well-known toilet preparation - which must surely contain some biological ingredients. What would a Brylcreme clone look like? Over to you, Stephen King.
