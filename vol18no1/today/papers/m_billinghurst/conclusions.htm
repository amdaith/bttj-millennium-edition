Out and About: Real World Teleconferencing

Conclusions

In this paper we have briefly described an augmented reality conferencing system that allows live virtual video of remote collaborators to be overlaid on the real world. We developed this prototype to overcome some of the limitations of traditional teleconferencing systems, namely the lack of spatial cues, the separation of real and virtual spaces and the requirement for users to be at their desktops. The main difference between this approach and others is that it allows remote collaborators to be 'taken off' the computer screen and placed into the real world.

Preliminary pilot studies among pairs of users show that AR conferencing may provide an increased sense of remote presence and better transmission of communication cues compared to traditional audioconferencing and videoconferencing. The results we have presented are confounded by the task chosen which encouraged the subject to look at the work space rather than each other. So further experimentation must be conducted over a range of different tasks. However, even if the AR conferencing provides results identical to desktop videoconferencing, this approach is valuable because AR conferencing can be used in a portable setting in ways impossible for traditional videoconferencing.

These informal trials have also revealed directions for research. In the future we plan to conduct experiments with more simultaneous users. It is expected that as the number of users increase AR conferencing will become even more effective because it can support spatial cues in ways that are impossible in traditional videoconferencing. Users could place remote collaborators around them in space and then turn to face each person as they talk to them, using the same gaze cues as in face-to-face conversation.

In our experiment we found that many AR users falsely assumed that the remote users could actually see from the viewpoint of their virtual video image. We plan to support this by adding small cameras to the user cards, which the additional benefit of allowing desktop users to have a view of the AR user's face. Another improvement is the use of spatial audio. When a user leans into an avatar representation their audio should get louder just as in face-to-face collaboration. Similarly when multiple people are speaking at once, spatial cues should be used to enable users to easily discriminate between them.

Our approach is particularly interesting in the light of recent developments in wearable computers. These are typically belt worn portable computers with a lightweight see-through head-mounted display. Several researchers have already shown how wearable computers are suitable for single user augmented reality applications such as navigation assistance [15] or maintenance tasks [16]. Although we are currently connected to desktop computers, we are in the process of porting our system to a wearable platform, enabling the user to collaborate from anywhere in the workplace and to have assistance with any real-world task.
