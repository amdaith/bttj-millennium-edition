Out and About: Real World Teleconferencing

Results

In general subjects had no problem completing the collaboration task. In both the audioconferencing and videoonferencing conditions subjects focussed mostly on the collaborative image viewing application, rarely looking at their partner in the video conferencing condition. In the AR condition, users wearing the HMD had more difficulty with the interface. They were able to easily position the remote user card and virtual whiteboard card anywhere in their workspace. Some were able to successfully place them so that both the remote user and the shared whiteboard were in view at the same time (figure 2). However, for others it was impossible to see both simultaneously. This was made worse by the small field of view of the HMD. Some users also spent a lot of time looking at the real pictures, covering up the AR tracking patterns with these pictures. This caused the remote user to flick in and out of view, as their card was revealed or covered up. In contrast, in the videoconferencing condition the remote user was always in view, even if only in the users periphery.

After each condition subjects were asked how present they felt the remote person was on a scale of 0 - 100, where 0 was that they were alone and 100 was that the person was in the room with them talking to them face to face. Table 1 shows the average results of this ranking for the 12 subjects who used the HMD in the AR condition. As can be seen, the mean presence for the remote collaborator in the AR condition is between that of the audio conferencing and videoconferencing conditions. There is a significant difference between these results, a one factor ANOVA finding (F(2,33) = 4.89, P<0.05).

Although these results contradict our first hypothesis, a different picture emerges when the amount of time the remote user was visible is considered. For some of the HMD users the remote collaborator was rarely if ever in view, while for others they were almost always in view. Thus for the users who rarely saw their remote collaborator, the AR condition was barely better than the audio-only condition, let alone being better than the videoconferencing condition. So it is to be expected that considering the entire pool of subjects in the AR condition would produce presence scores between that of the audioconferencing and videoconferencing conditions.

Another way to analyse the data is to divide the AR HMD subjects into groups according to what percentage of time their remote collaborator was in view. Figure 7 shows the average presence ranking for the remote collaborator compared to the percentage of time their virtual video image was visible. As can be seen the more visible the remote collaborator was the higher the presence rating.

Fig 7. Presence in the AR condition as a Function of Visibility

So taking the group of subjects for which the remote user was visible 75% of the time or more in the AR condition (4 of the 12 subjects) we can return to the presence scores and re-analyse the results. Figure 8 shows the overall presence ratings for these subjects for each of the conditions. As can be seen the AR condition now produces the highest degree of presence for the remote user. Despite the small sample size there is still a significant difference between these scores, a one factor ANOVA giving (F(2,9)= 4.46, P<0.05).

Fig 8. Presence Rating

Similar results were found for transmission of communication cues. Figure 9 shows how much the subjects agreed with the statement "I could tell when my partner was concentrating" (0=Disagree, 14 = Agree). There is also a significant difference between these results, a one factor ANOVA giving (F(2,9)= 4.41, P<0.05). Users felt that they were more aware of their partner in the AR conferencing condition. These results may be due to the size of the virtual video window (life-sized) and to the ease which it can be positioned anywhere in the real world, so users can see the remote person at the same time as their real workplace.

Fig 9. Concentration Rating

Subjects were also asked a related set of presence questions:

P1: How strong was your sense that the remote collaborator was really present with you?

P2: How completely were all of your senses engaged?

P3: How similar did you experience in the conferencing application seem with your face-to-face conferencing experiences?

These were answered on a Likert scale from 1 to 5, where 1 was Very Much So and 5 Not Very Much So. Table 2 summarises the average results. There is a significant difference between these results using a one factor ANOVA, as shown in the ANOVA and P-Value columns. Users felt that AR conferencing was more similar to face-to-face conferencing and provided a stronger sense that the remote collaborator was really there compared to audio-only and videoconferencing.

However, there were some disadvantages to the AR conferencing. The HMD had a narrow field of view (30 degrees), making it difficult to keep the virtual remote user in view and it was also somewhat encumbering and uncomfortable. The resolution of the HMD was also far less than that of the dekstop monitor. These effects compounded to make it take more effort to communicate in the AR condition. Subjects were asked to rate the overall effort involved in communication on a scale from 0 to 14, where 0 was Very Hard and 14 Very Easy. Table 3 shows the results. There is a significant difference between these results, a one factor ANOVA finding (F(2,24) = 3.94, P<0.05). Even though users felt the AR condition produced the greatest sense of remote presence, they also thought it took the most effort to communicate. We hope that future advances in unobtrusive high resolution HMDs will reduce these problems.
