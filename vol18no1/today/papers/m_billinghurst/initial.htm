Out and About: Real World Teleconferencing

An Initial User Study

Using the system described above, we have conducted several formal and informal pilot studies to discover how AR conferencing differs from traditional teleconferencing. In this section we describe the first of these studies. Prior to this study we observed that users found the experience of seeing a remote person superimposed over their world very compelling and behaved towards this virtual image the same as they would to a real person in their space. For example, users often positioned the virtual whiteboard and remote user so that they were both in view at the same time, attempted to make eye contact with their remote collaborators, and used the same gaze cues for turn taking common in face-to-face conversations. However, users also had difficulty with the HMD because of its limited field of view and resolution.

Thus in this study we had two hypotheses:

  • the life sized virtual images and spatial cues provided in the AR interface would lead to a higher degree of presence and communication awareness than either audioconferencing or videoconferencing,

  • the encumbering nature of the AR technology would make it more difficult to use than audioconferencing or videoconferencing.

To simulate a real teleconferencing experience we used a task where pairs of subjects had to negotiate about pictures. Subjects were told that they were to act as art buyers for a large art gallery. They had to decide together which three pictures out of a set of five the gallery should buy. All five pictures were by the same artist and in the same style. More importantly, they had to arrive at six reasons why those particular pictures should be chosen that they could use to justify their purchase to the art gallery director. These reasons could include a common theme, or colour, or any other reasons they could both agree on. This task was chosen because negotiation and conflict resolution has previously been shown to be sensitive to different forms of telecommunications media [14].

There were 12 pairs of subjects participating in the experiment, aged from 19 to 45 years old. Of these, there were six pairs of male subjects, three of females and three pairs containing one male and one female. Subjects did not know each other and were unfamiliar with the application and collaborative task. Each pair of subjects discussed which pictures to buy for an art gallery under each of three different conditions:

  • Audioconferencing (AC) - subjects were able to speak and hear each other using headphones and wireless microphones, and collaboratively view and annotate pictures in the simple desktop application (figure 6).

  • Videoconferencing (VC) - in addition to the audio connection and collaborative desktop application described above, a freely available desktop videoconferencing application was used to show live video of the remote collaborator.

  • Augmented Reality Conferencing (AR) - one subject used the AR interface described above, while the other used a desktop video conferencing interface. A virtual video image of the remote collaborator was attached to a card that the AR user could freely position in their environment and virtual images of pictures could be displayed on the shared whiteboard.

Each subject was also given a paper copy of the five pictures they were considering enabling them to see a higher resolution version of the images. The paper copy of the pictures was particularly valuable for the HMD user in the AR condition. Although they could not annotate the virtual images, they were able to hold the real pictures in front of the head-mounted camera and show by pointing with their finger the features they were interested in.

Before the experiment began subjects received training on how to use the desktop interface and also spent a few minutes in each condition with a dummy set of pictures. In the AR condition, both subjects tried the HMD and desktop interface for a few minutes so they could gain an understanding of what the other user was experiencing during the actual experiment. For each condition subjects were given 10 minutes to complete the task, although in some cases they finished ahead of time. A 'within subjects' experimental design was used so all subject pairs experienced all conditions, although the order of presentation and the images used in each condition were varied to reduce order effects.
