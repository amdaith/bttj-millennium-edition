Out and About: Real World Teleconferencing

Introduction

Computers are increasingly used to enhance collaboration between people. As collaborative tools become more common the Human/Computer Interface is giving way to a Human/Human Interface mediated by computers. This emphasis adds new technical challenges to the design of Human/Computer Interfaces. For example, although the use of spatial cues and three-dimensional object manipulation are common in face-to-face collaboration, it is difficult to provide the same capabilities in current desktop audio or video conferencing applications. Similarly, collaboration is often opportunistic and occurs away from the desktop environment and traditional computer support.

In order to support spatial cues, many research groups advocate the use of collaborative virtual environments; virtual reality (VR) can provide the same type of collaborative information that people have in face-to-face interactions, such as communication by object manipulation, spatial orientation, gaze, voice and gesture [1]. Work on the DIVE project [2], GreenSpace [3] and other fully immersive multi-participant virtual environments has shown that collaborative work is indeed intuitive in such surroundings. However, most current multi-user VR systems are fully immersive, separating the user from the real world and their traditional tools. While this may be appropriate for some applications, there are many situations where a user requires collaboration on a real-world task.

Other researchers are examining how portable computing can be used to support opportunistic collaboration, or remote assistance in a mobile environment. Network-connected portable computers can be used as communications devices to enable a remote expert to collaborate with the user. In collaborations on real world tasks the presence of a remote expert has been found to significantly improve task performance [4, 5]. However, current portable communications interfaces are rarely more than an extension of desktop interfaces, and do not support the spatial cues common in VR systems.

In our work we are developing interfaces which combine the advantages of VR and mobile computing to support real-world teleconferencing - teleconferencing which moves remote collaborators from the computer desktop and into the user's real environment. We achieve this through the use of Augmented Reality (AR), the overlaying of virtual objects on to the real world. In the past researchers have explored the use of augmented reality to support face-to-face collaboration. Projects such as Studierstube [6], Transvision [7], and AR2 Hockey [8] allow users to see each other as well as 3D virtual objects in the space between them. Users can interact with the real world at the same time as the virtual images, supporting spatial cues and facilitating very natural collaboration. For some tasks users collaborate better in a face-to-face AR setting than in a fully immersive Virtual Environment [9].

In this paper we report on an application of AR techniques for supporting remote collaboration. We have developed a system that allows live virtual video images of remote collaborators to be overlaid on the user's real environment. In this way we try to overcome some of the limitations of current audioconferencing and videoconferencing. For example, audio-only conferencing removes the visual cues vital for conversational turn taking, resulting in increased interruptions and overlap, difficulty in disambiguating between speakers and in determining other's willingness to interact [10]. In videoconferencing some visual cues are present; however, most non-verbal cues are not transmitted effectively and the lack of spatial cues means that users often find it difficult to know when people are paying attention to them, to hold side conversations, and to establish eye contact [11]. With our system, users can easily place life-sized live virtual images of remote participants about them in the real world and they can collaboratively interact with 2-D information using a shared whiteboard. This restores the spatial cues common in face-to-face interaction and provides a seamless interface between the real and virtual environments.

In the remainder of this paper we first give an overview of the technology used and then present results from an initial pilot study comparing AR conferencing to more traditional audio and videoconferencing.
