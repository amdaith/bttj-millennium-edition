The Future of Customer Contact

Customer doors into BT and the doors to the Future

As new technologies for access, such as multimedia kiosks, the Internet and Interactive Digital TV, become ubiquitous, customers will have access to an increasingly wide variety of possible interfaces with BT as part of their service. These are not mutually exclusive media. To the customer, choice is king.

The Telephone

The telephone is one of the major channels for customer contact now and is likely to continue as such into the future. Recent research from the Henley Centre indicates that customer's perceptions of service by the telephone are in decline. Centralisation of operations into call centres means that many companies have lost that local, friendly feel (figure 2). It is not sufficient to provide customers with fast accurate information and prompt solutions. The value is added by the relationship that the customer has with the person on the other end of the telephone.

 

Fig 2. The Call Centre of Today

The call centre industry is undergoing something of a boom with 1.1 % of the UK's working population currently working in a call centre and predictions that up to 9% of the UK's working population will be working in a call centre by 2005 [3]. However, the figures do not seem to add up when you consider that the industry average turnover per annum is around 30%. Unless the entirety of the UK's working population works in a call centre at some point in their career, there is going to a be a severe shortage of quality customer service people capable of delivering a quality customer interaction with the customer.

Technology offers a practical solution here in that mundane queries and routine interactions can be deflected away from the human customer service person and dealt with using Interactive Voice Technologies. Current Interactive solutions (e.g. 'for sales press 1, service press 2...') can prove more annoying to the customer than useful but a new suite of Interactive Voice tools which are capable of recognising and responding appropriately to the spoken word are beginning to evolve.

The role of the human customer service adviser is then transformed into a relationship builder and sales person. This means that the Customer Service Advisor of the future is employed as a good communicator rather than a person who takes mundane, repetitive problems and inputs them into a complex database of customer service information. These new breed of customer service people need systems which are dynamic and easy to use and provide them with the right information, in the right place and at the right time so that the customer relationship can be managed. It is likely that the call centre of the future will no longer be the 'new sweatshops' [4] but a dynamic, customer centric environment (figure 3, 4, 5 and 6).
 

Fig 3. Tomorrow's Centralised Call Centres

Tomorrow's Centralised Call Centres will provide facilities for high bandwidth multi-channel communication with customers. Wireless network technologies will allow Customer Service Advisors to work anywhere within the building (figure 3).
 

Fig 4. Localised Call Centres

Localised Call Centres within town and city centres will provide access to diverse customer markets and new types of Customer Service Advisor. Customer Service Advisors would work both at street level having personal face to face contact with customers and 'backroom' areas dealing with telephone, email and other higher bandwidth customer communication channels (figure 4).
 

Fig 5. Virtual Call Centres such as Telecottages

Virtual Call Centres will develop which utilise a teleworking workforce who operate from home and telecottages situated close to large residential areas.

Telecottages would provide local teleworking facilities for a number of Customer Service Advisors working for different companies (figure 5).

Teleworking Customer Service Advisors who work from home provide a call centre with a virtual workforce which can be called upon at a moments notice (figure 6).

Business guru Tom Peters has stated that front line people create revenue and should be treated in line with this revenue. Introducing motivation and incentive schemes allows organisations to officially endorse certain behaviours that are in line with the culture and service targets that the organisation wishes to develop. However, care must be taken that these 'official' motivators do not serve as demotivators for customer service people. Research from Bournemouth University [5] looked at how things that really motivate customer service people could be incorporated into the computer desktop (figure 8) as a Motivational User Interface (MUI). Electronically

By making systems more user friendly, customers can also gain direct access to BT's systems and serve themselves without direct human intervention (the concept of 'zero touch'). The availability of Internet access for customer service has essentially created what Peppers and Rogers [6] call an 'individual customer feedback loop' which integrates the production and service delivery processes into the research and promotion processes. More interactivity with the customer means more opportunities to learn about the customer. Tools such as Case Based Reasoning and Machine Learning can be used to interact with customers across all aspects of customer contact from provisioning, to fault diagnostics.

Access to customer service advisers to discuss further details is not precluded. Services such as BT's call-me facility provide a Web based front end to a call centre. By clicking on a call-me button on their browser, customers can talk to a customer service adviser using a separate phone line. Utilising simultaneous voice and data connections, agents can push Web pages to assist in answering the customer's query. The system can be configured as a virtual distributed call centre, or can integrate with existing call centre ACD equipment. This makes the experience of browsing more active, giving customers another avenue to contact BT and effectively blending telephony and electronic media.

Face to Face

The public face of BT to the average customer is the engineer in the grey van. They and the person on the end of the phone are the only major contacts that the customer will have with BT on an everyday basis. It is critical that these two customer service people provide a consistent customer experience and work together as a team to gather information from the customer and provide a quality customer interaction. The engineer is not a salesman but may be required to explain the features of the products and services that they are providing. Research looking at the relationship between members of what BT terms the 'Customer Service Team' is currently being undertaken with the intention of developing tools, processes and behaviours specifically focused on enhancing the customer experience. Another aspect to this is providing advanced fault diagnostic facilities to remote engineers in order to ensure that the engineer is only called out to a premise when absolutely necessary (figure 10).
