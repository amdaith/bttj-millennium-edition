The Broadband Access Facility

Introduction

The full service access networks (FSAN) initiative is a collaboration involving fourteen of the world's leading telecommunications network operators and major equipment manufacturers. Its vision, as shown in figure 1, is to create a shared requirements specification for access systems supporting narrowband and broadband services. This common specification will mean that broadband network components can be developed for world markets, thus raising volumes and driving down unit costs [1].

Figure 1 reflects the diversity of the different regulatory, business and structural environments faced by the individual members of FSAN, where operators are forced to deploy a variety of systems in response to operational needs and market drivers. However, sufficient similarities in architectures exist to enable significant benefits to be achieved in agreeing a common requirement [1]. The common system is based around an ATM/SDH (Synchronous Digital Hierarchy) core network, with local optical fibre distribution via an ATM PON. Digital Subscriber Line (DSL) systems maximise reuse of existing copper plant. The exact DSL system used depends upon where the optical system is terminated, e.g. in the local exchange, cabinet, curb or home. Hence this broadband access system can support a range of access architectures - this flexibility is fundamental to the consensus achieved in FSAN [1].

A broadband access facility at BT Adastral Park enables many of the different architectures proposed by FSAN to be tested. Initial work focused on developing the transport system and the interface to the customer [2]. In the last year BT has brought together ATM PON and VDSL systems from different manufacturers, developed a flexible transport infrastructure and focused on the end-to-end implementation of services [3].

  • FTTEx (Fibre to the Exchange) utilises a PON or other fibre distribution mechanism to provide connectivity from the national core network to the local switch or concentrator site. From there, customers within a few kilometres of the exchange can be connected at maximum rates of up to 8 Mbit/s downstream (800 K bit/s upstream) using the existing copper network with ADSL (Asymmetric DSL) systems. More typically, most telcos deploying ADSL will guarantee rates of around 2.5 Mbit/s downstream and 300 Kbit/s upstream to their customers.
  • FTTCab (Fibre to the Cabinet) relies on the PON being extended to telco primary cross connect cabinets to offer the higher bit rates of VDSL (Very high rate DSL) systems. This architecture is especially well suited to suburban deployment scenarios with relatively low customer densities. For low penetration scenarios a sealed unit know as the 'VDSL Brick' containing optical network units and VDSL line cards may be placed in the underground cable void. At higher penetrations a new VAP (VDSL Access Point) cabinet would be collocated with the primary cross-connect. The cabinet provides a concentration point so that customers can be served over existing copper twisted pair for the final 'kilometre' at rates of approximately 10 to 20 Mbit/s downstream, 2 Mbit/s upstream.
  • FTTK/FTTB (Fibre to the kerb/building) extends the concept of fibre distribution to a point at the local distribution point (kerb) or inside a customer's building. In dense urban areas customers dwelling in apartment blocks or business premises could have the fibre terminated in a basement and employ VDSL to reuse existing copper twisted pairs for data rates similar to FTTCab.
  • FTTB/FTTH (Fibre to the Business/Home) would require the complete change-out of the traditional copper network and would therefore represent a very high upfront cost for any telco contemplating deployment. At present only high-end business customers would seem to require this approach which has the potential in the future to deliver almost unlimited bandwidth.
