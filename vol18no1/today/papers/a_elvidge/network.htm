New Products for Old: Making a Profit

Network Evolution

Looking now at the second main purpose of the financial modelling work, that of supporting network evolution ideas, the main decisions refer to a first-cut assessment of the financial implications of future changes. These involve changes in any or all of the following:

  • service demand,
  • network architecture,
  • network capacity,
  • underlying technology,
  • routeing of calls across the network,
  • cost of capital,
  • network component capital and running costs,
  • indirect overheads and support systems costs, and
  • network component lifetimes and depreciation rates.

With the trend towards more intelligence-based services, calls are becoming significantly more complex beasts than simple telephone calls. The amount of processing required to set up and service a call is increasing, and in some networks is leading to exhaustion of capacity.

There are a number of remedies, for example replacing the technology to increase the power of the existing number of processing units (switches), or increasing the number of processing units. The latter option may well lead to a rethink of the entire network topology.

A range of broad-brush financial models has been developed to calculate financial measures with which to compare scenarios. The values arrived at are as accurate as possible, given the level of detail incorporated in the model, but their true value lies in comparing options on the same basis.

The Price of a Telephone Call

A financial model of the core telephone network tackles this wide range of 'what-if' problems. It builds upon a rigorous engineering model of the demand for capacity on the entire telephone network [5]. This model takes in demand forecasts for the next few years, which are agreed across the business, and maps them on to scenarios of network structure. The model takes into account the routeing rules for each service, the signalling flows, and the amount of processing power required to process the signalling messages to set up a call, at each type of switch encountered in the route. It also represents the processing ability of each switch type, and the traffic associated with each type of call. A rigorous dimensioning procedure then identifies the quantities of each type of equipment in the network, which are necessary to meet BT's quality targets.

The financial model interfaces to the engineering model (figure 9 ). The financial model takes in the information on the demand and equipment quantities for each year in the projection, and calculates what this means in terms of capital and current expenditure. The total costs of the network are then allocated among services in proportion to the relative amount of resources consumed by that service, and a unit cost per call minute is calculated. This unit cost forms a basis for wholesale prices, and as such is used to compare the effects of the alternative evolution scenarios attempted in the 'what-if' studies.

Fig 9. Capacity and unit cost modelling for the telephone network

Another measure of suitability, which is applied to identify 'good' evolution options, is based on free cash flows. Here the cash expenditure is calculated from the equipment requirements derived from the engineering model. The cash outflows are compared with the expected revenue, given the amount of traffic which can be carried successfully (customers are only billed by BT for successful calls). Measures of return on investment are then calculated, together with estimates of the profitability of individual lines of business (telephony, card, ValueCall, etc). The Solutions Business

An entirely new business paradigm involves mixing and matching a range of products and services to form a complete business solution for a customer.

This can be achieved by 'cross-platform' evolution, in which a platform is seen as technology which can deliver a number of individual capabilities, for example number translation, voice messaging, time-of-day routing. These individual capabilities could be reused and bundled together into packages to form an almost infinite number of combinations, tailored to customer requirements. Such flexibility and reuse necessitates the creation of software and hardware interfaces between platforms. It also raises the requirement that the company should have the ability to engineer the mix of capabilities in the most cost-effective and efficient way (figure 11).

Fig 11. Reuse of network components

This has led to the development of a cross-platform evolution model, which compares options for engineering a new service, using candidate capabilities from a number of different platforms. The model makes an allowance for the costs of building interfaces to bundle capabilities together into a complete service, and also represents the costs of building new capabilities from scratch, to add to existing platforms which lack the ability to support particular service features.

This model takes a broad-brush view of the engineering and dimensioning problem, but does give a 'quick and dirty' comparison of the resultant unit costs of the complete service package, given the different ways of creating it. Feasible options from a purely financial point of view are readily identified. Obviously, the financial aspect is not the only consideration; very often other factors, such as engineering feasibility, the obsolescence or limited future life of particular technology, or commercial contract terms and conditions, will rule the day.
