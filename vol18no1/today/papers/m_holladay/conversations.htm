The Value of Conversations

Once we start exploring models of the enterprises [4, 5], we uncover the conversations between the different roles. The attributes we ascribe to these conversations are significance, mutuality, informedness and control:

  • significance indicates the significance to each party of the conversation.
  • mutuality the measure of how shared or disparate the benefit of the conversation is to each party.
  • informedness how much information is brought to the conversation by each party.
  • control to what extent the conversation can be initiated by either party.

Within any given model, these attributes can be seen to take low or high values.

One of the benefits of valuing the conversations is that it can test the validity of the enterprise model proposed. As the structure of the model will influence the design of Operational Support, we need this validation to reject models that would lead to abortive development work.

The responsibilities that must be discharged by any role will impact the conversations in which they partake: if you have the responsibility "to ensure that the details of new customers' are correctly recorded", then you might want to take a high level of control. This might be achieved by using some sort of form to guide the conversation and ensure all relevant data is captured. The customer in this conversation will be more informed. This will enable them to take on the responsibility "to provide correct new-customer data".

Shifting Responsibility

Fig 6. Conversation Values

When we design Operational Support Systems, we assign responsibilities to roles. These roles may be undertaken by humans or machines. Where these responsibilities lie, in which enterprise and in which role, may be dictated by:

  • The nature of the service;
  • A conscious decision by the Service Provider to exploit differentiation in the market;
  • The need to adhere to standards;
  • Reaction to customer influence.

Moving a responsibility, particularly across an enterprise boundary, can change the value of conversation attributes. This will not only be on the conversation between the role with the new responsibility and another party, but also dependent conversations.

If we move the responsibility "to ensure that the details of new customers' are correctly recorded" from a role in the Service Provider enterprise to a role in the Customer enterprise we can see that this is the case. (In many Web-based services, the customer types in their own details.)

It could be argued that the SP is still has high control over the 'customer information capture' conversation, because the SP designed the form which 'allowed' the customer to enter data. However, one can soon see that the level of control has diminished - free format fields need to be provided so that the customer can use it as they need. Try getting a photo of yourself to display on your Yahoo profile, and you will see this is true.

. The impact has been on another (dependent) conversation. In this case (at least) two roles have reacted.

  • The role with the responsibility 'to display the profile' (the browser) must cope with missing data. To what extent this can be accommodated is agreed in conversations with standards bodies and interested enterprises like ISPs.
  • The role with the responsibility 'to transmit the profile page' (the Web server) will be extended to vet the data before transmission. Thus conversations within the Web service enterprise have been affected. The customer database is regarded as less informed by the data dispatcher (WWW Server) than was the case when the responsibility to get it correct was wholly within the Service Provider.

Other examples that exhibit these changes in conversation value can be drawn from models that, for example, move the 'responsibility to calculate the bill' to a 3rd party.

Whether any laws can be derived which govern these conversation values is still the subject of research. It is clear, however, that understanding these values, and how they change, can help us understand the OSS requirements.
