All Channels Open: Multimodal Human-Computer Interfaces

Previous research on multimodal interaction with computer systems

Here we present a brief survey of previous research on multimodal interaction with computer systems. A useful substantial survey of the field of multimodal speech systems may be found in Benoit et al [12]. This contains over 400 references, and includes work on face-tracking, gesture recognition, etc, as well as multimodal integration.

Real-time multimodal perception and action

Thorisson [13] produced a multimodal dialogue agent called Gandalf, implemented in an architecture called Ymir. Ymir is wide-ranging and ambitious, but its chief focus is on enabling real-time interactivity, so that, for example, the agent is able to respond by gesture and facial expression as the user is speaking and gesturing, and in between turns, thus creating a much more lively interaction.

One modality affects another

What happens to speech when we introduce another modality, for example a pen allowing simple gestures and handwriting? Applications which manipulate spatial information show a change in the linguistic tokens and structure used. Being able to point to a location of the screen and say "Put a Platoon here" is far simpler than saying "Put a Platoon at co-ordinates 953, 445." Conversely, the addition of speech to a graphical interface dispenses with navigation around menus, etc, by giving a flat structure to the system's functionality. Linguistic complexity decreases, task time improves and so does 'naturalness' (see for example, Johnston et al [14]).

The effect of experience

Is it natural and intuitive for users to use multimodal interaction? How will a novice user interact with the system? Petrilli [15] suggests that the subject's experience influences the pattern of behaviour. A form-filling system using pen and speech input showed that inexperienced subjects used more than one modality in an 'utterance' only 30% of the time, compared with 84% by those with previous experience. This suggests that system interface designers cannot expect a user to be able to walk up to a system and use it optimally for the first time, unless they had previous experience of such interfaces. Experienced subjects used the additional modalities to reduce the linguistic content in their interaction. Wizard of Oz simulations by Carbonell [16] showed a great deal of variability in interaction styles not only from one user to another, but also for the same user conducting different, but similar tasks. Carbonell reports that novice users found multimodal interaction difficult without additional instruction. Whether this is because the inappropriate 'metaphor' is applied to the application is unclear. Certainly the experience of the user is an important factor and it would be interesting to determine whether a wrong metaphor has been applied, for example a 'desktop' metaphor, leading to difficult interaction.

Designing for error

Although common practice in other areas such as Human Computer Interaction, 'designing for error' is not as prevalent in multimodal interface design, perhaps because such experimental systems are built as vehicles for research into discourse structure and so forth. The ability to use speech recognition presents the problem that users do not know the sub-language required, and because of its 'hidden' nature, the range of functionality present in the system [16]. Although there has been a great deal of literature about 'linguistic convergence', that is where the participant adapts his or her language to the capabilities of the dialogue partner [17], there are often entrenched patterns of behaviour over which the user has little or no control. Oviatt [18] suggests methods to overcome two such behaviours - disfluency and hyperarticulation - through careful interface design where error handling is in-built. Certainly given the current state of the art, interfaces which employ speech recognition need to be 'designed for error'.

Disfluencies are common in spontaneous speech and contribute greatly to low speech recognition accuracy. Many types of disfluency, for example, false starts, verbatim repetition and filled pauses, cannot be consciously controlled by the speaker and currently systems which attempt to compensate for this effect use language-modelling strategies, which have not been particularly successful to date. For example, research by Shriberg [19] focuses on identifying acoustic/prosodic cues for detecting self-repairs in conjunction with syntactic, semantic and pattern-matching information. An alternative approach is to design the interface so that disfluencies are minimised though the use of multiple modalities. Oviatt found that the greater the cognitive load, as prompted by unstructured tasks and the need to describe complex spatial information, etc, the greater the disfluency which occurs. By designing an interface so that the task is structured by the system and not by the user, and introducing a pen to relay spatial information, disfluencies can be dramatically reduced.

Another problem common to speech recognition systems is that of 'hyperarticulation'. This is the over-articulation of an utterance, typically following a speech recognition error. The speech models used by the system are not trained for this elevated articulation, leading to further misrecognition. Oviatt calls this problem a 'spiral of errors'. One method suggested to compensate for this is to swap out the speech models once hyperarticulation has been detected with models which are trained on appropriate speech. Unless the task is well-structured, automatic detection of hyperarticulatory speech is difficult. Another approach is to design the interface so that the user can make use of other modalities if speech recognition fails. Oviatt [18] shows that users can intuitively switch to a different modality after persistent misrecognition.

Some remaining questions concerning multiple modalities

The above issues raise a number of questions regarding the integration of more than one modality. One of the most pertinent questions is which are the dimensions that influence the use and combination of modalities. For example, how important is the temporal relationship between gestures and speech. Intuition tells us that a gesture should occur within a time window of the utterance to which it relates. What is the temporal relationship between the gesture and the utterance and to what degree is it critical? Such issues may be of more relevance when multiple touches are combined with a single utterance.

Is there a natural bias towards one modality over another? Given speech and gesture, will speech always be prominent and gesture only used as a form of linguistic efficiency? Or do modalities have an equal weighting? This may depend on the domain, the degree of experience of the user and the robustness of the mode of communication.
