All Channels Open: Multimodal Human-Computer Interfaces

Approaches to multimodal integration

Here we examine in detail the approaches to the integration of more than one modality which we intend to investigate through the implementation of our fully automatic system. After an initial discussion of modality integration and a comparison of holistic integration with the more traditional unification approach, three versions of the latter are discussed: speech-driven, template-based and event-driven.

What is involved in modality integration?

The challenge faced by multimodal systems is not the technology - individual technologies from data gloves to directional microphones are slowly improving - but the integration or synergy of the differing modalities. Co-ordination and integration of multiple input streams allows humans to communicate fluently and we appear to do this seamlessly. Replicating this fluency within a computer system is the challenge currently facing designers of interfaces and dialogue management systems.

Events, such the touching of an object in the virtual room, are concrete occurrences; their semantic interpretation must be determined through context. It is the role of the touch interpreter to receive a discrete touch event, and then form a number of possible hypotheses on the actual meaning or intention of the user. For example, the user may touch the sofa in the virtual world, that the user has touched the sofa is definite but it is unclear without taking the context into account whether he or she is referring to the actual sofa or the fabric on it. Thus the touch interpreter must generate a number of hypotheses regarding the user's intention. Similarly, multiple interpretations can apply to speech, especially given the error-prone nature of speech recognition. The Natural Language Unit can formulate hypotheses based on multiple recognition of a word, on multiple meanings of words, and on the context of the domain. The role of the modality unification module is to take the multiple semantic interpretations and deliver a combined interpretation, if possible.

Holistic Integration

The two main approaches to interpreting the combined meaning of a number of inputs are unification based and a form of convergence or holistic approach. The holistic approach takes the notion that modalities have a direct influence on each other and that an interpretation of the interaction is based on a form of consensus where the interpretation of each modality is influenced by the interpretation of the others. Such a system was proposed by Sharma [20] where the components of speech, gesture, gaze and a model of the virtual environment directly influence each other's interpretation. While their system takes surface elements from one modality to constrain another, it is unclear whether it would be valid as a more integral approach using deeper information, due to the differences in the number and type of dimensions that each modality occupies. Speech is one dimensional, gaze is two or possibly three dimensional, as is gesture. Such an integration of modalities is certainly an interesting research area, however.

Frame Unification

The most common approach is to use a form of frame unification. The inputs are interpreted independently of one another and then combined to find an overall interpretation. In the domain of spatial manipulation, at least, Oviatt et al [21] has shown that pen gestures such as handwriting and pointing typically occur before speech and can overlap, and that gesturing occurs within a 4-second window of the spoken reference. With this in mind, Johnston et al [14] uses a semantic-based unification strategy where each modality is treated with equal importance, rather than relying on speech to be the main modality. Within an interaction window, input is collected from each modality and individually assessed. This information forms a semantic frame representing the input. Individual feature-value pairs in Johnston's system are type-constrained and there is a typed place-holder for the location at which the platoon will be created. The modality integrator awaits input from other modalities for a fixed length of time and then attempts to unify the frames. Each modality can produce an n-best hypothesis list of interpretations of the input - so, for example, if decoded speech is ambiguous, the interpreter can output a list of the most likely candidates. Unification is a method of contending with the ambiguity and errors which occur in each modality. Through unification, many hypotheses can be eliminated, leading to hopefully one or a small subset of possible interpretations. The success of such approaches appears to vary, however. A system by Vo and Wood [22], for example, uses a similar approach of semantic frame merging for calendar-based appointment booking, but allows dialogue history to further constrain and interpret input. Confidence measures for each hypothesis were combined from multiple modalities and the best chosen as the correct interpretation. Vo and Wood ran experiments at first using a Wizard of Oz simulation to provide accurate handwriting/gesture and speech recognition, but found the integration engine correctly interpreted the input with only 80% accuracy. Further experiments using actual recognition software lead to accuracy ranging between 35-62%, partly due to the poor performance of the handwriting recognition component. Perhaps it is the complexity and broadness of the domain which primarily contributes to the poor performance, rather than the technique of semantic frame merging, indicating that this simple approach may not be adequate for similarly complex applications.

A variation on the frame-merging method discussed above is proposed by Siroux et al [23], who uses rule-based merging to understand an interaction. Unlike Vo's system, tactile information takes precedence over speech, but dialogue context is again used in the interpretation. The model is similar to that proposed by Allen [24], where a Communicative Act (CA) is viewed as an operator for a plan. Speech, for example, produces a dialogue act with place holders for deictic terms; touch produces an event of a specific type. If the body of the CA is applicable, given that the place holders are instantiated with information of the correct type (i.e. touch), then the CA is activated.

We will explore three approaches to unification: speech-driven, template, and event-driven.

Speech-driven unification takes a semantic interpretation of incoming speech and using this to form the basis of a template. This template is then used to interpret other events, such as touch. For example, if the user said, "Put Cartouche Stripe on this" and then touched the sofa in the virtual world, then the semantic interpretation of the speech would be along the lines of [function:transfer, fabric_name:'Cartouche Stripe', deixis:[cat:dem]]. Further events would be applied to this template, so that the touch event would be resolved to [function:transfer, fabric_name:'Cartouche Stripe', deixis:[cat:dem, type:object, value:'Sofa']].

The main advantage of this approach is that it formulates a local context as soon as it has understood the speech and can then apply the incoming events to this context. This can limit the number of interpretations possible, since the hypothesis space can potentially grow exponentially.

There are a number of theoretical arguments against this approach, however. The main argument questions whether there is a natural bias towards using speech in all interactions; this approach relies on always having speech accompanying other gestures to interpret the user's intentions. In general this may not always be the case, since many gestures are more efficient and less ambiguous than the equivalent speech can be. Should more weighting be given to one modality over another? This may be domain and task dependent. Given the ambiguity of speech and the frequent grammatical errors made by speakers, it is the combination of speech with gesture and other modes which tends to make interaction more robust. Speech-driven templates assume that speech recognition of spontaneous speech is robust, and today's speech recognisers are not accurate enough to solely rely on for the basis of the interaction.

Template unification relies on a number of domain and task-specific structures which can be applied either as each event is received by the module or after a specified amount of time (a time-out window). The MUeSLI system would rely on a template for each function that was possible. For example, there would be a 'transfer fabric' template. Each template is applied in turn, top-down, to the set of event hypotheses and a ranked set of template hypotheses returned to the dialogue manager.

An advantage of this approach over speech-driven is that it need not place emphasis on one particular modality and considers each modality's semantics as support for each template. Taking such a top-down approach implies an interpretation of the user's intentions which may be beyond the scope of the modality interpreter; instead this responsibility should lie with the dialogue manager which is able to interpret the breadth of interactions based on the local context. Indeed, such interpretation may only be possible by the dialogue manager. Another disadvantage of this approach is that a template is required for every type of interaction, including those in sub-dialogues, for example when the system is eliciting further information from the user.

Event-driven unification regards each event hypothesis will equal weight and attempts to combine each incoming hypothesis with the current set to form a new hypothesis which unifies one or more others. For example, after the user has said, "Put it on this", he/she touches the sofa in the virtual world; when this touch event is received by the modality interpreter, unification is attempted. In this instance, the deictic reference 'this' will unify with the event hypothesis, 'sofa', giving a full interpretation of 'Put it on the sofa'.

This approach does not rely on one particular mode of interaction, as does the speech-driven approach, and the combination of different events can lend weight to the reliability of any interpretation. For example, if two touch events are received, along with a speech hypothesis that has little confidence, then the unification of all three can lead to confidence in the interpretation. Unlike the template approach it does not apply a top-down interpretation of the meaning of the interaction, but rather passes a number of ranked hypotheses. Hence, the module can be regarded as a 'modality parser', which defines what is a legal combination of modalities, similar in respect to a natural language parser. The weighting can ensure that only the hypotheses which combine all of the incoming events are passed on, and failing that, the ones that incorporate the greatest number of unified hypotheses. One disadvantage of this approach is that the event hypothesis space can grow exponentially as new events are interpreted. This could be a problem for a system which had ambiguous input, for example did the user mean the sofa or the fabric on it when he or she touched it? Other contributing factors include poor speech recognition resulting in a large number of hypotheses and interactions comprised of a medium number of modality events. In the MUeSLI system this may not present a problem since legal inputs are restricted to a maximum of three input events, for example in the command to transfer a fabric on to an object: speech + touch + touch. Interpretations will fill this space and the largest factor will be the number of interpretations returned by the speech recogniser.

Event-driven unification raises the following issues.

  • Two events may only be related by the fact that they occurred within a time window; without further unifying 'glue', how should these events be interpreted? For example, in the MUeSLI system, the user may try to 'drag and drop' a fabric on to an object. Is this a legal interaction, or do we insist on always having further information which can unify them - in this case the spoken command? If we do allow this interaction, then there is only the time window in common - do we pass a 'loosely unified' interpretation (hypothesis) to the dialogue manager, or two unrelated hypotheses?
  • How are the confidences from each event hypothesis combined? There is obviously scope to include confidence values from the speech recogniser and in future projects, other modalities.
  • What is the correlation between the timing of events and the time that they are actually received by the modality integrator? This issue relates to that of polling, that is where some event manager triggers the modality integrator to examine combined hypotheses after a certain amount of time has passed and pass any that are relevant to the dialogue manager. Given the relatively long decode time of speech recognisers it is likely that touch events will arrive after speech even though they may have occurred during or after speech. The short answer to this is to give each event a relative timestamp; however, how does the system deal with the following situation: a touch event arrives, the system waits out the time window, but then receives decoded speech which is timestamped to within the window? When the system has timed out, it has polled the modality integration module to send on any viable hypotheses to the dialogue manager - in this case the single, touch event.
