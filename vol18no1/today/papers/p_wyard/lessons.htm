All Channels Open: Multimodal Human-Computer Interfaces

Lessons Learned and Ongoing Work

We have not yet compiled and processed all the experimental data, so our findings are still somewhat general. The types of lessons we have learnt may be summarised as follows.

  • Through the focus groups, the Wizard of Oz simulations, and early reactions to the fully automatic system, we have learnt much about user behaviour with multimodal systems.
  • We have gained experience of how to build a multimodal system which actually works.
  • Through an accompanying programme of formal experiments, we are 'teasing out' answers to some of the more detailed questions, e.g. what determines user preferences for different synthetic personae in different applications.

Having completed a working automatic system, the current drive is towards greater robustness, greater intelligence and co-operativeness in the system. Robustness is of key importance for an effective user interface - it is no use having an interface which is very powerful and natural as long as you know exactly how to interact with it, but otherwise falls over. Each single component in the system must be made more robust, and the integration of components must be improved to cope with the inevitable lack of component robustness which will remain. By robustness we do not mean reliable software (which is taken for granted). Rather, we are talking about something which requires further research and engineering at the level of speech, language, AI and virtual world technology. A brief discussion of three components, the recogniser, the DM and the NLU module, will give an idea of what is meant.

Robustness in the recogniser involves greater robustness in the face of background noise and speaker variability, a much broader statistical language model (while still having good predictive power), and a richer channel to the NL server, so that more possibilities can be passed forward and decisions deferred to a later stage in processing the user's input. Robustness in the dialogue manager involves the ability to cope with a less programmatic style of interaction, with users able to be less sequential and straightforward in describing the tasks they want done. Whether this requires a modification to the current dialogue task objects module will only become clear after user trials. The NLU component is already very robust, but may need to be modified to do some less localised parsing and thus remove some of the ambiguity which it currently passes up to the DM.

As far as co-operativeness is concerned, the current MUeSLI system is able to make suggestions, such as which fabrics go well with the user's selection, and therefore add to the sense of 'having a conversation'. We intend to increase the system intelligence, enabling it to be much more proactive, and to work co-operatively with users to achieve their overall goals, such as to choose a good design (according to certain criteria) and/or to stay below a given price. This will be reflected in a much more mixed initiative style of dialogue, i.e. the system and the user will be able to take turns in leading the conversation, in a natural manner. The initiatives will be perceived as coming from a dialogue partner with the intelligence to understand the whole flow of the conversation, rather than simply to make isolated suggestions.
