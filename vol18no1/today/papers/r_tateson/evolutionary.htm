Nature-inspired Computation: Towards Novel and Radical Computing

Evolutionary Computation

In the previous section, we looked at a nature-inspired solution to a particular problem. In this section, as the second example of nature-inspired computation, we discuss a large class of techniques known as "evolutionary computation". These methods have been applied to a wide range of problems and an applet is included below to show how mobile network planning might be approached in this way.

Evolutionary computation is one of the most powerful nature-inspired computational techniques (reviewed in Bäck et al [20]). It encompasses a number of evolutionary algorithms, including genetic algorithms [5], genetic programming [6], evolution strategies, and evolutionary programming. These techniques were originally inspired by Natural Selection (or 'Darwinian' Evolution) where populations of individuals vie for limited resources. Over many generations, individuals which perform better in their environment tend to produce more offspring. Since offspring inherit characteristics from their parents, with small mutations, this selection mechanism leads gradually to fitter individuals. In evolutionary computation, a fitness function is usually defined which makes explicit how well a given individual solution is performing a desired task. An individual may be, for instance, a program whose task is to retrieve relevant information from the World Wide Web. Alternatively, it may be an encoding of a particular network design, where the fitness function includes the various network cost components.

Figure 5 shows an 'Evolution Strategies' algorithm performing a specific task. A snapshot of the best current individual in the evolutionary population is shown to the left, and the corresponding fitness of that individual at each generation is plotted on the right. As the individuals in the population find better solutions the fitness increases correspondingly. In this particular case the individuals each encode a fixed number of circular regions of coverage. The task, which is rewarded by higher fitness, is to cover as much of the available area as possible, whilst minimising overlap. This is a simplification of a problem relevant to a cellular mobile telecommunications problem; namely that of obtaining good signal coverage whilst minimising regions of interference. Note that this problem is complementary to the channel allocation problem described in the previous section. Whereas the channel allocation problem relates to the channels which will be used by base stations in a network of fixed topology, this problem relates to the optimisation of that topology (either when creating a new network, or when building new base stations to add to an existing network). In other words, optimising channel allocation can help you do better with what you've got, but when you're committed to building new base stations, you want to know where best to put them.
 


Fig 5. (a) An Evolution Strategies algorithm - The task is to evolve a solution with good coverage of the region indicated, while minimising areas of overlapping coverage. In this case the individuals each encode a fixed number of circular regions of coverage ('aerials'). The task that is rewarded by higher fitness is to cover as much of the demand as possible, where the colour intensity in the map indicates the demand. There are, however, a few restrictions: there is an upper limit on the maximal demand that each aerial can cover, overlap of aerials should be minimised and there is a cost associated with each aerial relative to its size. This is a simplification of a problem relevant to cellular mobile telecommunications, namely that of obtaining good signal coverage while minimising interference and costs.

Evolutionary computation is a very exciting area with much promise for the future. Already algorithms have been successfully applied to difficult design problems such as network design [21] and bandwidth allocation. Work on genetic programming [6] is seeking to automatically generate software to perform certain tasks; currently this does not scale well to large programs, although it can be demonstrated to work on small programs. The issue of what contributes to the evolvability of a system is currently a hot research topic to overcome the scalability problem [22]. As these factors become better understood it opens the possibility of beginning to automate commercial software and system design, as well as to tailor systems to specific user requirements, with huge cost saving potential.

Moving research forward in such a broad field as evolutionary computation can be a frustrating process. The twin goals of pursuing important principles, such as evolvability, and working on applications, such as greenfield network design, can both involve a lot of 'donkey work' producing the test environment in which the 'evolution' will take place. Research into new techniques, and application of those techniques, would be speeded up if this process of creating a world with physical and genetic rules could be facilitated. For this reason we have developed a powerful evolutionary and ecosystem tool-kit called 'Eos' (Evolutionary Object Store). This is an extremely flexible system, written in Java for portability, which supports the major classes of evolutionary algorithms in addition to ecosystem models. The tool-kit extends these in various ways to allow rapid prototyping of novel evolutionary algorithms and to support their application to specific problems. In other words, an à la carte Eos world can be quickly created, allowing more time to be spent on the problem-specific aspects - the dynamic interactions of individuals, the effect of different rules and starting conditions, the properties of different genome encodings and so on. The example evolutionary system shown in figure 5 is implemented using the Eos tool-kit. Eos also provides a framework for current and future research into areas such as co-evolutionary systems, and computer immune systems (for security applications).
