Nature-inspired Computation: Towards Novel and Radical Computing

Computers and Software in Crisis

Almost every computer in the world is based upon the Von Neumann architecture [1], depicted in figure 1. In this model, programs and data are stored in memory and can be accessed or written to by the Central Processing Unit (CPU). The CPU executes the stored program sequentially, one machine instruction at a time. While the architecture performs magnificently on tasks which are inherently serial or which perform simple, repetitive arithmetic tasks, it is now clear that it is not nearly so suited to certain other domains. The traditional architecture and algorithms begin to show weaknesses in problem domains where tasks are highly parallel and asynchronous, or involve a significant degree of pattern matching.


Fig 1. The traditional Von Neumann architecture, used almost exclusively in modern computers in which programs and data are fetched from memory and the CPU steps through each instruction code one-by-one.

Attempts have been made to tackle some of the more difficult problems by other approaches and computational metaphors such as neural networks [2, 3], and fuzzy logic [4]. In part due to the difficulty of designing and programming solutions to the 'less suitable' problems, new approaches to programming based on evolutionary techniques such as genetic algorithms [5] and genetic programming [6] have been developed. All of these techniques have proved helpful in the 'tool-kit' of available algorithmic problem-solving techniques, and yet we still find difficulty tackling the more complex, parallel, asynchronous problems which cannot easily be rendered as a sequence of simple instructions governed by if-then style rules.

In addition, current computer systems and algorithms are extremely brittle and prone to catastrophic failure [7], often caused by tiny errors. Examples include:

  • instances of near-complete telecommunications network failure ('brownout') caused by small, simple errors in large, complex software packages [8],
  • failure of multi-million dollar space delivery vehicles [9, 10, 11], or aircraft, due to software bugs and procedural errors,
  • regular operating system crashes on desktop PCs due to components interacting in unforeseen and unpredictable ways.

This situation arises partly due to the lack of fault tolerance in current systems. It is more likely that a simple software bug will cause a serious error or system crash than have little or no effect. This raises the question of whether we can conceive of alternative architectures or algorithms which, by virtue of their implementation, are more forgiving of minor errors, imperfections, or noisy data.

Current computer systems are still predominantly designed as centralised solutions to problems, even in those cases where the problem itself is de-centralised by nature. This probably reflects our inability to easily conceive of distributed solutions to problems. As information systems and networks become more complex, centralised solutions do not scale well and the likelihood of unwanted interactions between large numbers of system components rises. The associated costs clearly rise significantly too, as more computer programmers need to be employed to design the software and to support and maintain the system. While current advances in computer hardware, software, and algorithms will help us incrementally cope with more complex systems, they seem unlikely to match the rapidly increasing demand for software, or tackle the issue of system reliability and the desire for reduced maintenance costs [12].

Therefore, in addition to investing time, money and effort in improving current practice, it is sensible to broaden horizons and look for radical new methods, principles, and even paradigms, for computation. The natural world contains many examples of systems with desirable features such as autonomy, robustness, adaptability and dynamic problem solving. The work presented below was all inspired, at some level, by methods, principles and paradigms found in nature.
