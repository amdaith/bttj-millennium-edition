The Embedded System Millennium Bug - Did it get you?

Assessing the Scope

The way most companies and countries tackled the problem of identifying their embedded systems was to find and identify them all first. That was not an easy task because they could be anywhere. But like all tasks, if split down into smaller sub-tasks, it became more manageable. Here local knowledge of where everything was likely to be was invaluable, especially as the embedded systems were located in the most unlikely locations.

A consistent database was also required - how many engineers can admit to correctly spelling such company names as 'Hewlett Packard' and remembering the two 't's. Likewise, where companies have changed name or merged, the equipment might be the same, but the name badge may be different, e.g. 'GPT' 'Plessey'.

Once every possible embedded system had been identified, catalogued and identified in some way, there was a system in place to monitor the movement of the units and the spares inventory of such equipment. This was very important especially if external maintenance contractors had been used. Probably the best way most companies may have found was to use the old and tried 'Traffic Light' approach. Firstly using a RED tag for when an embedded system was identified, and as testing/Y2K compliance was made and assessed, this sticker was replaced by an AMBER or a BLUE sticker showing partial/full Y2K compliance. It was the convention in most companies to use BLUE stickers for compliance rather than GREEN - BLUE indicating minimal risk rather than 'safe'.

Yet, even trying to identify these Embedded Systems was problematic. After all, what did you look for? Thankfully, the IEE book Embedded Systems and the Year 2000 Problem [9] had always been a useful starting point for identification, asking:

  • Are there any flashing lights.
  • Is there a display (LED or LCD).
  • Are there any buttons or switches.
  • Is there a battery backup.
  • Does it control any valves, motors or systems.
  • Are there any signal cables connected.

    With hindsight I perhaps would have added the following

  • Was an electronic date displayed? This was probably the biggest give-away, but quite often it was just cosmetic.
  • Were there any time/date-related calculation made, e.g. averaging, statistics? Perhaps not so obvious. Remember the famous case of the Corn Beef tins with their '00' sell-by date.
  • Was the year displayed as 2 or 4 digit representation? Even when the year was shown in full, the '19' portion of the year may have been hard coded and would never roll round to '20'
  • Did the unit connect to an external receiving aerial? Not quite so obvious, but it might have indicated the use of an external clock or time of day source e.g. MSF [10], DCF77, GPS [11]. (which only transmits a 2-digit year code. GPS had other problems on the 22 August 1999.)
  • Was there a 'hidden' maintenance mode for the service engineer?
