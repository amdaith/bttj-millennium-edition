The Embedded System Millennium Bug - Did it get you?

Failure Modes

These embedded chips or systems failed in one of four ways with either a temporary, partial, permanent or catastrophic malfunction:

  • Temporary malfunction:
    At the 1999-2000 year rollover, the system 'stopped' working or 'locked up' in some particular way (much the same as our PC computers 'lock up' in daily life.) This was due to the internal functionality of the embedded software code not being able to cope with a calculation or procedure that crossed the year-end boundary. However, once into the 21st century and the system restarted, the code was able to function correctly, and no fault was apparent. Care needed to be taken at the time as the system might also not have recognised that the 29 February was a valid date and cause a similar 'lock up' condition to occur.
  • Partial malfunction:

    Here the embedded system carried on functioning correctly, but processed the date and/or time incorrectly. For many systems, the time/date display was there for reference purposes, and the fact that the display did not show the correct data was of no consequence. It may have been possible in some cases to set the year portion of the date back 28 years to 1972 where the days of the week coincide with those of the year 2000. Providing everything else functioned correctly, there should have been no problem running the system with an incorrect date unless there are perhaps some legal implications, or the system was attached and reported to other processing equipment for further computational or statistical analysis.

  • Permanent malfunction:

    • In this case, there was just no functionality in the embedded chip after 1 January 2000. However, if the date information was relatively unimportant, it may have been possible to set the year back to 1972 when the date and the days of the week match that for the year 2000. This of course assumed that no permanent damage had been done to the embedded chip, and the year could be set back to 1972. It was generally believed that very few (if any) cases fell into this category.

  • Catastrophic malfunction:
    This perhaps was the one that we were all worried about. Here we were expecting the software to function in an unpredictable way, possibly breaching safety protocols and causing death or injury. Fortunately for us, this scenario did not occur. We are still here. However, there could be longer-term consequences if embedded systems at the bottom of a chain have failed and are unable to fully report their status in the hierarchy of the system, causing a failure later in the year.
