The Embedded System Millennium Bug - Did it get you?

Abstract
 

By the time you read this article, you will either not be here to read it, or the disaster postulated by the 'prophets of doom' never happened. This article hopefully aims to try and bring the Year 2000 problem or Millennium Bug into some form of perspective and ask the question, 'could this problem happen again'.

For the past couple of years there has been a major concerted effort by almost every company in the world to determine, and if necessary correct computer code that could not cope with the 1999 to 2000 century or Millennium rollover. The so-called Millennium Bug.

Firstly, let's bring this Millennium bug into perspective.

Over the past couple of centuries it has been our custom when dating any document to represent the year by a simple 2-digit code. When the date passes through a millennium, i.e. from 99 to 00, most people by common sense are able to interpret the year change correctly (1799 - 1800, 1899 - 1900 etc.). With the advent of digital stored program computers in the late 40s, this shorthand notation for indicating the year portion of the date was similarly used, primarily because this type of date nomenclature took up a smaller space in computer memory. This was especially important because in those days, computer memory (RAM) and storage media (Tape) were at a premium.

Since that time, much software code has been written, and software engineers being what they are, have been quite happy to reuse code and software routines they have used in the past that manipulated the date character field using only a two-digit representation. The net result is that almost every company in the world has found that at the beginning of the next century, many of their computer software packages and embedded systems or chips will not function correctly because they process the year rollover incorrectly. An embedded system at the end of the day being no more than a small computer that has been programmed for one or a small number of specific tasks (examples: video recorder, traffic lights, central heating timers etc.)

But what sort of number of embedded chips are we talking about?

It is estimated that there are about 24-25 billion (that's 24/25 with 9 '0's) embedded systems in the world. With the world population of about 6 billion that's about 6 embedded systems for every man, woman and child on this planet. It has been estimated by computer experts (US Computer Industry) that about 2% of these embedded system are non-compliant. Some estimates have put it as high as 5%. That is still 10 billion plus devices that will need to be found and if necessary replaced. When you are talking these sorts of numbers, one obtains an impression of the task in front of them.

This article aims to take a forward look into the 21st century to forecast the Year 2000 problems that could possibly occur and speculate as to what might happen in future millennia.
