Large vocabulary

"Please say the surname"

Large vocabulary dialogues are the next step on from spoken menus. They may be imagined as menu-based systems where the menu is too large to list. These huge 'menus' are typically proper nouns such as surnames, towns, roads or account codes. This style of dialogue is often called 'data centric' as the application normally uses speech to access a large database of information, for example automated directory enquiries. Although this overcomes the problem of menus, it introduces new difficulties such as coping with modelling homophones and synonyms, e.g. 'Smyth' and 'Smith', 'Dave' and 'David'.

Large vocabulary speech recognition is rapidly improving but it is still error prone. Questions in large vocabulary systems are more open than in menu systems, sometimes encouraging verbalisation by the caller. Whether such verbalisation is a factor of mirroring prompt style, or uncertainty about what speech is appropriate is not known. It is therefore important to pay particular attention to the wording of the questions to try to get callers to say the data items in isolated phrases as much as possible and not to encourage them to use sentences (see Grammer-based natural language). For example "Please say the surname" will usually get an isolated response whereas "What's the surname" will get responses preceded by "its.." or "the surname is..". A detailed discussion of many of these speech input issues may be found in Attwater et al [4].

One study that tested this type of interface was a flight enquiry service. Flight enquiries are good examples of large vocabulary applications where callers can present their request one item at a time when prompted for it. Due to the error-prone nature of large vocabulary recognition, confirmation of the details is often required by the dialogue before the enquiry may proceed. This experiment investigated the effect of different prompt intonations on the caller's ability to detect and correct errors during confirmation. A trial service was designed which deliberately simulated recognition errors. The errors made by the service were manipulated, so that in some conditions the confirmation given was accurate, but in other conditions there were errors. The results show that the style of confirmation can be critical to the way users respond. When there are no errors, most users will give a clear 'yes' or a synonym to the confirmation. However, when errors occur, the behaviours change. For a statement style confirmation (e.g. 'From X to Y on DATE'), a proportion of users remained silent in spite of the presence of an error. For a query style confirmation (e.g. 'From X to Y on DATE?') all users responded in some form either saying a simple 'no' or synonym or spontaneously correcting the error.

These findings show that even in a machine/human interaction, users are still acutely sensitive to the intonation of a prompt, and that this can seemingly have a greater impact than the wording of a message, at least as far as error correction is concerned. When this is compared to psychology research, the parallels are remarkable. Mehrabian [5] found that human communication is 7% language, 38% paralanguage (e.g. intonation), and 55% visual cues. When using a telephone based-service, visual cues are absent, so users are left solely with verbal and paralanguage information to guide them. Given Mehrabian's estimates, it is therefore not surprising that paralanguage has an important role in conversational grounding even in situations where the caller knows that they are not conversing with a human.
