There's Nowt so Queer as Folk!

The drive towards perfection

The dramatic shift towards telephone-based transactions in recent times has led to a plethora of new automated services emerging. These typically provide call steering and self-service applications in place or, in support of, a human operator. Most telephone services currently use recorded prompts and simple menus which allow callers to use the telephone keypad (TouchTone) to access services. Speech recognition technology, however, is maturing rapidly and accurate recognition over the telephone network of vocabularies of thousands of words and simple sentence structures is now feasible. As this technology matures the style of interface which can be supported becomes considerably more flexible, as we endeavour towards more human/human-like conversation.

Fig 2. The different generation of dialogue styles

Figure 2 shows the different generations of voice interface that are in common use or under investigation at the current time. The precise nature of these dialogue styles will be described later, but they show a continuum that tends towards normal human conversation. As the continuum proceeds, faster more featured interactions can be achieved which are more usable and acceptable to the caller. However, this brings with it increased design complexity and heightened caller expectations. Callers experiencing an interface which appears to be more and more like a real person react by demanding equivalent understanding, resulting in language and behaviour from the caller which are increasingly difficult to predict.

Attitude towards automation

In order to establish what people really want from spoken interfaces a series of focus groups have been conducted. Three groups of 6-9 people, balanced for age and gender, and socio-economic status were selected.

The predictors of attitude towards automation are complex. In general, however, it appears that people are wary of current styles of automation - which in the UK is almost exclusively TouchTone. The following comments from focus group members illustrate some of the current negative attitudes towards automation:

"I phoned {company X} and it took ages. It goes from one to the other, then it repeats, then I said it wrong so it went back, its not quicker. I must've been on there for three or four minutes; I was really annoyed."

For many users the advantages of automation are elusive. They frequently encounter systems designed around organisational requirements, with no consideration for the callers' perspectives, so it is hardly surprising that we find such low expectations of automated systems. It is interesting to note that people are aware that there is often an emotional content to a telephone enquiry and people believe that this cannot be fulfilled by an automated interaction:

"I would prefer to talk to a human being. It's the reaction you get to an emotional response - everybody needs it."

People are able to abstract - to describe the type of service from which they would perceive benefits, but the implication is they have yet to encounter one:

"Probably a machine has got more knowledge than a human being anyway, hasn't it? So it's probably better and quicker."

Other answers strongly focused on personal control and a natural spoken interface. The desire for control was pretty universal, with the ability to quickly break out to a person being a common requirement. Speed of response, accurate recognition and full understanding were also strongly desired. Some participants felt that automation could not be acceptable under any circumstances.

Until the all-too-common negative experiences are counterbalanced by positive interactions with automated services, it is unlikely that attitudes towards automation will change, and people will continue to dream of the day when they can talk to a machine and receive service equivalent to that provided by a fellow human.

The paradox is that when told that one day automated systems might be indistinguishable from a person, there was almost universal agreement that people still wanted to know that they were talking to a machine:

"You'd have to be told. I mean it would be nice if it was good enough to actually be able to understand to a degree that it would be like talking to a real person. You'd still want to know"

There was common agreement that it would be humiliating to talk to a machine thinking that it was human, especially if it became obvious later in the conversation that this was the case:

"I don't think it matters as long as you know that it's a machine. If it sounds clear and it's like a human it doesn't matter, but if you don't know and you start chatting you'd feel really silly."

Others recognised the inherent contradiction in the desire on the one hand for completely natural interactions and on the other to know that they are conversing with a machine:

"It's a catch 22 because other people would say that they want the machine to sound more like a human. You get to a stage when you've got it exactly and we say 'no we don't like that.'"

The speech race

From the focus groups it was clear that current automation solutions are just about usable but not well liked. People's expectations are driven largely by what they already know, but when shown examples of more natural interfaces their objections become more sophisticated. The authors term this effect the 'speech race' reflecting the almost endless potential (until machines are just like people) for spoken man/machine interfaces to require more and more complexity and functionality as caller expectations are raised. There are of course practical and technological reasons why this escalation is not particularly desirable nor even sustainable. For this reason it is important for dialogue designers to understand what they want to achieve and to select an appropriate level of technology. It is even more important for callers to clearly understand the capability and limitations of the system with which they are currently interacting.

The remaining sections consider each dialogue style in turn and highlight, using an example for each style, the importance of designing for the real behaviour of people.
