There's Nowt so Queer as Folk!

Robust natural language

"How can I help you?"

What happens when callers are simply asked "How may I help you?" and no constraints are put on them to behave in a certain manner? This is the holy grail of speech and language technologists, and in some cases may be the only practical way to automate an application. This has been found to be particularly true for telephone helpdesks where callers are generally infrequent and they are looking to present a problem to the agent. They often do not have a carefully formulated idea of what they want when they call.

For example in a recent study of BT's operator service helpdesks [7], 49% of callers specify a problem, they do not request a particular service; 17% ask the operator to do something but do not actually use an explicit service name; 32% explicitly ask for a particular service and 2% talk nonsense! Language usage can be difficult to predict, and callers are also very disfluent - ums, uhs and restarts (e.g. 'rev… reverse charge') of words are common.

Successful techniques for addressing this kind of problem have focused on the use of statistical language models for speech recognition. Statistical language models are generally learned from a large number of examples rather than hand coded. In contrast to grammar-based natural language this freedom of expression makes it more difficult to extract meaning from the spoken input in order to decide what to do next in the dialogue. It is interesting to note that a large proportion of calls to helpdesks are relatively simple to resolve once the problem/request has been correctly identified. Also the range of problems may be relatively few even if the language used to describe them is complex. This means that current approaches generally use robust topic identification - again learned from a large set of examples. This statistical approach lends itself well to task identification and frequent problem specifications. Once the task is identified, more traditional grammar-based approaches can be used.

Robust natural language interfaces are the subject of ongoing research and current language technology is not mature enough yet for many applications. Within relatively well-coped problem areas, however, it is now becoming feasible. The case study below presents some of the results from a recent investigation into customer handling using robust natural language.

Robust natural language case study - tone of voice and wording on customer service helpdesk.

In order to investigate the human issues of robust natural language a Wizard of Oz experiment was run on live traffic on BT's 100 operator service. [7] This is the same service that was used for the spoken menu case study presented above.

One concern of the experimenters was to encourage speakers to talk to the machine when they expected to get an operator. In order to investigate this, different welcome prompts were tested, where the wording and the formality of the speech style was varied. One of the prompts ("Operator services speaking") is identical to that used by human operators, and acted as a calibration against the human/human performance.

Table 1. Proportion of 'unco-operative' responses to initial prompt for different wording and speech style

Table 1 shows the proportion of calls which produced an 'unco-operative' first response in the caller for two of the prompt wordings with two tones of voice. An 'unco-operative response' was typically silence but also included the caller querying whether they had reached an operator, or exasperation at hearing an obviously automated system. As a control, analysis of the human/human corpus showed that approximately 10% of first responses were classed as uncooperative.

As can be seen from table 1, the speech style of the prompt had an enormous effect on the initial caller response. Note that the second prompt wording, which is regarded by UK dialogue designers as more formal due to the word 'welcome', has slightly worse performance than the more personal first prompt. Even in the very best case, there are more unco-operative responses than the 10% found in the human/human corpus, possibly indicating some residual indication of 'professionalness' in the recording. From this experiment, it is clear that the style and nuances of the initial recorded greeting require very careful attention to elicit co-operative responses. Research is ongoing to determine if the language use is still consistent with the human/human corpus.

This result has significant implications for the remainder of the dialogue in a call routeing system. Firstly it suggests that the highest quality speech output is needed in these applications, which still requires the use of pre-recorded messages, limiting the flexibility of the dialogue. This result corroborates the fact that paralingual cues are important in man/machine dialogues. It also suggests that callers do not have a model of how to respond to an automated system, yet from the focus group results it was clear that callers do want to know that they are talking to an automated system. This is a central paradox in the design of natural dialogues for naïve callers. As speech recognition performance improves, speech output may become the limiting factor in holding highly natural interactions with customers.
