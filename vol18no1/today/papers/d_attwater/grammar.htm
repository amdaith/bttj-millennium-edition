Grammar-based natural language

"What do you want to do now?"

Grammar-ased natural language systems make the interface more natural than large vocabulary dialogues by permitting callers to use simple sentences to say what they want. Callers may speak more than one piece of information at a time, e.g. "I want a flight from Washington to New York today please." Callers may now describe a task in a single sentence, dramatically increasing the speed of the transaction. Consequently callers may say almost anything when given such an open question as 'What do you want to do now?'

Fig 5. A simplified example of a speech recognition grammar

Current speech recognisers require an apriori grammar of possible sentences which may be spoken at each stage in the enquiry, and these are usually expressed with a context-free grammar. Figure 5 shows a simple example of a context-free grammar for a question such as "how often do you want the reminder call?". The dialogue must be described in such a way that it can handle all possible permutations that may be observed. The design complexity of such systems can increase exponentially if the designer is not careful [6].

Consequently, grammar-based natural language systems are usually most successful with frequent callers who can learn the admissible sub-language and dialogue. The behaviour of callers even in this instance is difficult to predict and it is important to do live trials in the most realistic possible environment to discover what people really will say at various points in the dialogue. By understanding how users behave at different points in a human/human dialogue, we can better understand what grammars and dialogue structures may be employed. It also helps provide clues as to how users correct mistakes in a natural way (contradiction) and what the precursors are for requiring a sub-language mechanism (help).

Grammar-based natural language case study - flight enquiry service

In order to investigate the issue of grammar preference, a Wizard of Oz trial simulating a flight enquiry service was built. This service allowed people to specify a particular flight route and date and then gave additional information about the flight. Triallists could choose to say all of the information in a single sentence or could give it in separate parts.

Participants made three successive calls to the system using flight scenarios which were presented graphically using a diary page to minimise priming bias. The first two calls had either one substitution error introduced or no errors introduced - distributed randomly and equally. The final call had multiple errors introduced. Figure 6 shows the number of pieces of information (date, destination and origin) that were given in the first sentence response to the question "please say your flight details". Results showed that users tended to habituate towards giving more information in a single sentence as their usage of the interface increased.

Fig 6. Number of information items given in sequention calls

These results can be explained by a number of factors. Firstly, as users were repeatedly presented with the task in the experimental condition, it is likely that the aims of the task, and therefore their understanding of their goal in the interaction, became clearer. This would have assisted users in their interaction with the service. Secondly, repeated exposure to the service enabled users to learn the most effective interaction style, such that by the final attempt, they would have a better understanding of what to say to get best performance from the system. Finally, as users heard the prompts repeatedly, they would have transferred the prompt information to long-term memory, thus reducing the cognitive load while interacting with the service. More detailed study would reveal the key behaviours that facilitated such learning, and show which of the varying strategies users adopted was most successful.

Importantly, this study demonstrates that designing a service that gives users opportunities to learn from their experiences without being penalised for errors has clear benefits for repeated usage. It also demonstrates that the way callers use a service for the first, and for subsequent calls, will change, and that an interface must be able to accommodate this elegantly so users do not suffer.
