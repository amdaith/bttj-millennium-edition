Spoken menus

"Please say reverse charge, faults, sales or directory enquiries or hold for an operator."

Spoken menu dialogues follow a simple question and answer format which permits callers to answer with one of a small set of isolated phrases. The menu wording can have a direct association with what the caller wants and can therefore be more intuitive. For a speech service, providing prompts that state key words encourages users to mirror this interaction style, and produce speech that is more likely to be successfully recognised.

Some of the key design issues are selecting the wordings that will elicit such mirroring, and determining the order of item presentation. We know that short-term memory exhibits primacy and recency effects, so this can be used to the designer's advantage, but encouraging users to cut-through prompts is another matter. In spoken menu systems in the UK, first time dialogue users tend to wait for the end of a prompt before speaking. This means that they must remember their choice for the duration of the menu, with the risk that the new information in each sequential option will displace the memory of the item they need to select.

Navigation through a series of menus is also an issue. Men generally have better spatial ability than women, which can assist when dealing with such a task, but women are often more proficient at dealing with multiple sources of information, which can also be advantageous. Another issue is age of the user, generally young people cope better with menus than older people, but it is hard to say whether this can be attributed to more efficient cognition by the young, or more confidence with using technology. Perhaps both have their role.

Some of these issues have been investigated with the use of live trials at Adastral Park to discover the optimal designs that suit the way that people really are.

Spoken menu case study - BT operator assistance call screening menu.

A recent study has investigated the use of a simple spoken menu to perform call screening on the front of BT's '100' operator assistance service in the UK. This service receives over 200 million calls per year and is primarily intended for people who want operator assistance with connections. The number is memorable, however, and callers often dial it in preference to the correct BT service, such as customer service or faults. For this reason a simple menu to screen out inappropriate or self-service calls was investigated in a live trial.

Given the huge call volume, a small percentage difference in the efficiency of the solution can make a big difference in cost saving. For this reason the menu efficiency, the proportion of people who want a particular service who actually respond to a particular menu item, was extremely important. It was believed that the location of menu items in the spoken menu list would affect the efficiency of that item. For this reason, the study investigated whether the most popular menu item should be at the head or tail of the list. A four-item menu was used to investigate this using live traffic from callers who were not expecting an automated service. The two most popular options were reverse charge and directory enquiries. Therefore two menus were recorded. The first had reverse charge offered first, and directory enquiries last and the second swapped them over. The two middle menu items were not altered.

Figure 3 shows the 'menu efficiency' of these options, depending on whether they were offered first or last in the menu. Menu efficiency is defined as the proportion of callers who responded to a menu item when they wanted something in the menu. This result came from sampling only those calls where the caller wanted either reverse charge or directory enquiries.

 

Fig 3. Efficiency by presentation order of menu items (95% confidence interval shown)

From this graph, it is evident that 'Reverse charge' was selected much more frequently when it was offered first than when it was offered last. The error bars show the 95% confidence bands either side of the result. There were too few directory enquiries calls for an equally confident conclusion.

Therefore, for menus which default to an operator if callers remain silent, menu items placed at the start of a four item menu list are more likely to be selected than if placed at the end. For an option that is known to generate a majority of calls, the increased selection rate can have considerable financial impact. This illustrates how understanding the relevant aspects of human psychology can make a significant difference to the final efficiency of a particular solution.
