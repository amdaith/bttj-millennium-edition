There's Nowt so Queer as Folk!

TouchTone

"You now have three options. For sales please press 1, for billing please press 2, for all other enquiries press 3"

TouchTone dialogues, although disliked in many instances, are now almost ubiquitous across the developed world, often forming the front line of customer service systems. TouchTone interfaces are essentially very simple, but do not provide an intuitive interface. They limit callers to one of twelve buttons to express everything that they wish to do. This can be very demanding and poor design can quickly lead to almost unusable systems. In the context of human communication, TouchTone is the equivalent of shopping in a foreign country, without being able to speak the language. The only way of communicating one's desires is to point across the counter and hope that what you end up buying matches the appearance in the window. Oh what disappointment to arrive back at your villa with a chicken pie when what you really wanted was the apple turnover with cream.

The very nature of a TouchTone interface means that all too often the capabilities of users are forgotten. While technology improves, the capacity of the human brain remains largely the same, and if such limitations are ignored, trouble ensues. The first issue is the difference between brain function and IQ. Intellect is a construct applied for social judgements, and is measured by criteria determined by psychologists, who openly acknowledge that the applicability of a number generated by a test is, to say the least, limited. This leads to the common, but misguided belief, that a service aimed at, for example, business executives, will be usable with ten options in a menu, eight levels deep, simply because business executives are clever. It is unfortunate for the business executives presented with such systems that their intellect may not parallel their memory capacity, their spatial ability, or the speed with which they can process audio information.

Studies by Miller [3] of short-term memory span have shown that most adults can remember a series of seven items, with a range of plus or minus two. Applying this to a TouchTone menu, users are presented with an option and its associated number, in a sequential list. To succeed, the user must remember which option they require, and the number they must press to reach it. This means that a menu with three choices translates to 6 items to be remembered, closely hitting the average of most people's ability.

Add to this that while the user is listening to the menu, they are also conducting other tasks. For example, deciding the relevance of each option to their end goal, co-ordinating holding the receiver and pushing buttons, and also distinguishing the prompts from other sounds, perhaps station announcements, television noise, or a barking dog. For more complex services, users must also keep track of where they are in a series of menus, knowing how to return to options they may need, or deciding how to cope when they make a mistake. And so, it is common for a badly designed TouchTone interface to generate a cognitive load that even a clever person cannot surmount.
