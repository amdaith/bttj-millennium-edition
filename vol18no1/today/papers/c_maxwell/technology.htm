The Future of Work: Understanding the Role of Technology

The Role of Technology

Information and communications technology is facilitating these changes. It allows much routine work to be automated and easy storage of huge amounts of information. It offers new possibilities for accessing information from anywhere and interacting with colleagues regardless of where they are physically. It is an integral part of the future of work. The technology is available now to completely revolutionise the way that people work but, in practice, behaviour is likely to change more slowly as it takes people time to understand how to harness its potential. Technology canot only change the use made of a centralised physical workplace but also allow people to go beyond these boundaries and still work effectively by communicating with others and accessing information.

Centralised Working

New workplace design must support flexible use of space based on changing tasks. The traditional layout of offices allows individuals to personalise their workspace but encourages them to be territorial. The workspace can reflect an individual's status. Such a fixed space allocated to each person is no longer sufficiently flexible and can be very expensive, particularly in city centre offices. Recent schemes such as British Airway's Waterside have been designed to support employees working anywhere in the building, as they feel is appropriate [9]. This means access to the corporate Intranet from anywhere and the ability for incoming calls to be routed to the individual person rather than a physical location. Cordless technology is already being introduced to allow workers to move around more freely. Arthur Anderson's 6th floor suite in the Strand is another recent, high profile office of the future. Desks are clustered in groups of three, for use by anyone, but, compared to a traditional office, they take up a relatively small proportion of the total space. Other zones have been designed for different activities: Zen, a quiet area, Chaos, an area for brainstorming, and conversation rooms.

Dispersed Working

A central physical workplace is only one of the environments where work takes place. Remote access is possible both to individuals within the organisation and to vast amounts of the organisation's recorded knowledge.

People are no longer tied to one place but can work on the move. Increasing miniaturisation of electronic equipment and the availability of wireless communications links have freed people from using a desk at all. Reliable remote access and mobile communications are therefore critical to supporting workers effectively in the future.

Technology to support relationships

Communications technology can readily support basic one-to-one interaction and conferencing between individuals. Although computer-mediated communications cannot duplicate the richness of face-to-face communication, these alternative media have different strengths which may compensate for this. They are becoming increasingly effective for supporting a whole range of tasks and a wider range of collaborative work activities will be possible remotely, making use of documents and other shared resources in a virtual working space. This is technically possible now, but many challenges remain if such systems are to be fully exploited.

The first key issue is that technology redefines the possibilities for how people are controlled. As direct supervision is no longer feasible, a new approach to management control is needed. Remote monitoring of computer usage is possible but risks alienating individuals from the management system, which imposes this. Control through monitoring output is more acceptable but still has problems if the nature of the work is difficult to define and changes rapidly. The second key issue is how to integrate physically dispersed individuals into a team to avoid isolation and encourage creative interaction.

Technology to manage knowledge

Knowledge has now been recognised, more than ever, as a critical resource within an organisation, which needs to be managed effectively, whether it is recorded or held in individuals' heads. There are several aspects to this problem. As much of the organisation's corporate knowledge is retained within the memories and skills of individuals in the workforce, the value of such people needs to be acknowledged. Secondly, electronic information storage removes the old constraints of physical storage space and allows people to accumulate large quantities of data. This becomes increasingly difficult to organise in such a way that vital information can be retrieved quickly when needed. Thirdly, the ease with which information is accessible makes it harder to cope with the sheer volume of it. Individuals need help in dealing with this overload of information. Lastly, giving workers access to corporate information, wherever they are, helps people work effectively but also poses security risks, which need to be dealt with.
