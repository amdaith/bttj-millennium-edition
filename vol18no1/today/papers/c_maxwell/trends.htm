The Future of Work: Understanding the Role of Technology

Trends in the Future of Work [1]

Organisations are constantly seeking to become more efficient and innovative in order to keep ahead of the competition. Globalisation has pushed back the boundaries of the world within which individuals and companies operate. Such a dynamic environment is complex, uncertain and demanding. At the same time, the decline of traditional manufacturing and the growth in service industries has caused a fundamental shift in the type of work which needs to be done. Increased automation means that fewer people are required to participate in or oversee the production process. There have been many changes in the way that people work in the 1990s and, facilitated by new information and communications technology, the rate of change is likely to quicken over the next decade.

The changing nature of work

Defining work can be problematic. It is usually considered as an activity by which individuals support themselves financially. It traditionally involves payment for providing a good or service. Work is often the way that individuals get a feeling of self worth and achieve status in society. It frequently requires attendance at a place of work. Such assumptions are now being challenged, due to changing social attitudes and the potential offered by new technology. More flexible forms of working are inexorably replacing the traditional form of a job. There is currently a public debate on how the government and society should ease the transition towards this new world of work, spearheaded by the RSA (Royal Society of Arts) Redefinition of Work Project [2].

The future will never be totally predictable but some trends have become clear over the last few years. As organisations pursue flexibility, the virtual business has become a more common form of organisation. More individuals are adopting flexible working practices, adjusting both the time and place where they work. A significant area of change is the growth in the importance of knowledge work. This is closely associated with the move to a service economy. This is clearly changing the lives of individuals in our society, as pressures increase to learn new skills and be more flexible and responsive, throughout our working lives. The prospect of individuals having a job for life, as we have known in the past, is diminishing.

The changing structure of the organisation

Virtual businesses are organisations which extend beyond the traditional boundaries of ownership. They may be transient in nature and members may simultaneously belong to several virtual organisations. Such organisations are responsive to the complexity and fluidity of the economic environment. They often rely on networked computer systems and computer-mediated communications to interact and operate. They offer the opportunity for a very different working environment to the traditional bureaucratic organisation.

These changes bring benefits but they also bring new problems. 'The flexible, dynamic, customised organisational form is here' [3] but such organisations must face up to these challenges. In particular, the organisation must counteract the tendency towards physical dispersal and individual autonomy and give some cohesion to its members. Team spirit is normally established through the informal relationships which result from working in the same location. Charles Handy feels that future organisations will be just "boxes of contracts" [4]. What matters, in the outside world, is the brand image. A strong corporate culture and effective leadership act as powerful unifying forces. These are necessary to counteract the tendency for individuals' interests to diverge. People will be the organisation's greatest asset and should be valued as such. Well-rewarded, motivated individuals, doing work which fits well with their skills and personal goals, are likely to be more loyal, and contribute more effectively to achieving the organisation's objectives.

Closely related to this is the need to achieve a balance between centralised control and empowerment of individuals. Any automation of work processes can be designed to either impose a rigid centrally controlled workflow or, alternatively, to support flexibility and local autonomy. The move towards a remote workforce is one of the drivers pushing organisations towards control by monitoring output. The main difficulty with this approach is that it does not fit well with standardised work practices which often form the basis of automation. A strong, pervasive corporate culture can also be used as an indirect control mechanism. Organisations must somehow manage the paradox of retaining control of their workforce while simultaneously empowering individuals in order to encourage flexibility and creativity Flexible Working People are now expected to work more flexibly in order to respond to customer needs - 74% of large organisations currently have formal or informal flexible working arrangements in place [5]. Flexible working is a general term which encompasses flexibility achieved by a number of mechanisms.

Flexibility may simply be in terms of when work is carried out. In the UK, 21% of full time and 24% of part time employees worked flexible hours in 1997 [6]. Clerical and secretarial workers make most use of this type of flexibility. It encompasses a wide variety of schemes including flexi-time, term-time working and job sharing.

Part time work and the use of short-term contracts allow organisations more flexibility in resourcing their operations. Part time workers account for 25% of the workforce in 1997, compared to 22% in 1987 [6]. The growth in temporary work is discussed in 'Jobs for Life' below.

Teleworking gives additional flexibility by allowing people to work remotely. Only 4% of workers can be said to be full-time teleworkers but around 22% of the work force work from home at least some of the time [5]. In the UK, over 60% of all people who work mainly from home do work which is classified as secretarial, administrative, managerial, professional or technical [6].

Over and above the number of people officially teleworking there are an increasing number of workers who rely on communications technology to interact with their colleagues and managers. Many mobile workers are rarely in their official workplaces. Alternatively, the workforce may be in a single location but geographically separated from their manager. In effect, due to transport and telecommunications technology, time and space are no longer insurmountable barriers to communication. This has removed the necessity of being co-located in order for people to work together effectively.

Knowledge work

The importance of knowledge work has grown in parallel with the shift to a service economy. The knowledge worker is concerned with the manipulation and processing of information to produce knowledge rather than physical artefacts. The Warwick Institute for Employment Research forecasts that the number of knowledge workers (as opposed to secretarial/clerical, manual or sales/personnel services workers) in the UK will increase to over 10 million by 2001 [5]. Given that the number of economically active people in the UK labour force in 1998 was just under 28 million [7], this shows how significant knowledge work has become. The recognition of this fact by organisations has led to the current focus on knowledge management as an issue of critical importance. The importance of knowledge work is creating a demand for a more educated workforce with individuals who continually build on their knowledge and skills. The knowledge retained by the individuals and systems within the organisation is proving to be one of the most critical resources in the future and it needs to be managed effectively.

The impact on individuals

'Work content becomes increasingly dynamic and cross-functional' [3] as organisations seek to be responsive to the demands of the market-place. Different skills and abilities become important in this new world of work. Skills in problem solving, creativity and self-reliance are more important than ever. Interpersonal skills increase in significance, as short-term project teams become more common. The ability to build effective working relationships quickly will be very important, especially when physical dispersal makes informal communications more difficult.

The improvement of knowledge and skills will need to be a continuous process. 'The premium is not on slow and gradual learning but rather on continuous, relentless accelerated learning' [3]. Organisations will be expected to support their workers in this process but individuals need to take more personal responsibility for themselves and their career development, as lifetime employment with one company becomes rarer.

As the world of work is no longer constrained by time or place, it becomes increasingly difficult for individuals to maintain a boundary between work and home life. This is already a common problem for teleworkers. Individuals have to learn to define their own limits effectively.

Those in work are likely to be under extreme time pressures and to have increasing difficulties in balancing the conflicting demands in their lives. In 1996-7 three fifths of married couple families were supported by two income earners, compared to half in the early 1980s [6]. In 1998 the number of men in employment had increased by 5% since 1986, whereas the number of women in employment had increased by 15% [7]. The most marked increases are in women working part time and women with children aged under five. The percentage of economically active women (either working or seeking work) is expected to continue to get closer to the percentage of economically active men. Forecasts estimate that, by 2011, 58% of all women will be economically active, compared to 70% of all men [7].

There is a widely held belief that continuous employment for everyone is not a realistic prospect. Only 53% of 12 to 25 year olds expect to be in permanent employment with either the same employer or consecutive employers [8]. Temporary employment currently accounts for 7.8% of all jobs [5]. The increase has been particularly marked for men. The proportion of temporary male employees in the UK nearly doubled between 1990, when it was 3.7%, and 1997, when it reached 6.6% [6]. Freelance working and voluntary work are also more likely to play a role in an individual's working life at some stage.
