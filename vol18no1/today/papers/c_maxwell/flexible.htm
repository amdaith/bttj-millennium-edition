The Future of Work: Understanding the Role of Technology

Flexible workers and trust

Trust is a particularly important issue in relation to managing a remote and flexible workforce since, in these circumstances, managers do not necessarily see their staff on a regular basis. Frequent, informal, face to face interactions are the best way to encourage the development of a good working relationship, based on trust. These interactions occur naturally between workers and managers based in the same office environment. Flexible workers are normally reliant on the telephone and computer mediated communications to interact with their managers and colleagues so they must overcome any potential impact, which this may have on their working relationships. In the future, this pattern of working will be more widespread. Identifying the factors, which enhance trust and those which threaten to undermine it, is therefore an important step in preparing for this future.

The ABC trust model was used to analyse an example of a flexible working scheme in order to identify important factors related to trust. The model was used to highlight the factors, which encouraged or inhibited the development of a good working relationship between employees who were flexible workers and their managers. The model also structures these factors into Antecedents, Behaviour and Consequences.

The outcome of this particular flexible working pilot study was known to be positive, in that the scheme was continued and extended. This case study was used to validate the model for completeness. It gives some useful insights into the factors, which are important in encouraging trust. The key points of the case study findings, relevant to the relationship between manager and staff, highlighted by the trust model, are outlined below. This analysis has been discussed in more detail in an earlier paper [12]. The implications for the future of work will then be considered.

The case study involved fourteen software engineers, many of whom were originally based at the same location. Some of their colleagues remained office based after the introduction of the flexible working scheme. Even in this situation, where a high degree of trust already existed, there were still some concerns. The key questions related to trust between the flexible workers and their managers were as follows.

  • What factors were important in enabling managers to trust individuals to work effectively without direct face-to-face supervision?
  • What factors enabled individuals to trust managers to treat them fairly compared to their office-based colleagues, whose activities were more visible?

The model was used, in retrospect, to identify these factors at the point when the flexible working pilot scheme was begun. The consequences of flexible working were examined in the light of the model's predictions after the scheme had been extended and had been in operation for 18 months. The factors relevant to each of the components of the model are described below.

Antecedents

Trust antecedents are made up of innate (or dispositional) trust and the situational context. Together these summarise the current status and history of the relationship between the flexible workers and their managers. Innate trust is inferred from the natural tendency of the individual or organisation to trust others. Situational factors, such as the nature of the task and knowledge of the partner, combine with this innate trust to produce an overall level of trust.

Innate trust

Personality, attitudes and culture all contribute towards innate trust. These can be affected over time by the individuals' experiences. The flexible workers in this study had all worked with their managers for a significant period (between two and ten years) before the flexible working scheme began. Consequently they had already developed trusting relationships, enabling them to work successfully together. The individuals worked autonomously without significant supervision but met frequently with colleagues to catch up on news and local issues.

Feelings of trust were facilitated by positive attitudes towards the scheme. The flexible workers had volunteered for the scheme and felt respected at having a choice about their work styles. The managers recognised the commitment given to the flexible working scheme by these individuals. Nearly all of the individuals had been involved in setting up the pilot scheme. The managers felt that their main challenge was to ensure that both the flexible workers and their office based colleagues all continued to feel part of the team.

A significant factor of the new working culture was that the flexible workers would be able to choose where and when to work, depending on their tasks, other work commitments and family circumstances. The fact that managers trusted the flexible workers to operate in this way, without constant supervision, is indicative of the degree of trust that had built up prior to the flexible working scheme.

Situational Context

Key factors in the situational context which affect the managers' trust in the flexible working arrangements are the types of tasks performed, the competence and autonomy of the individuals concerned and the quality of the technical support. The individual workers' tasks in this case were predominantly software programming, analysis and design. They also had to write up reports, attend meetings and liaise with customers. The established nature of the relationship between the workers and managers meant that there were no concerns about individuals' competence to do the work required.

Individuals already worked relatively autonomously. The control mechanisms which were already in use, based around the definition of personal objectives, regular counselling meetings and annual performance appraisals, were used for both the office based and flexible workers. The decision not to introduce additional control mechanisms for flexible workers reinforced the positive attitude towards the scheme, as they felt that they were trusted to do their job, regardless of their location.

Personal incentives make individuals more inclined to accept a certain level of risk. For flexible working to be successful it has to offer benefits for both workers and managers. All parties in this situation had a personal incentive to make the scheme succeed. Managers wanted to reduce costs and increase productivity and flexibility. Individuals wanted more flexibility about when and where they worked in order to reduce the amount of travelling they needed to do and increase the amount of time they had for themselves or their families.

Behavioural Elements

The ABC trust model shows that expected behaviour is derived from a combination of subjective trust (made up of innate trust and the situational context), the perceived risk and the importance of the outcome. In this case the risks identified by the individuals were

  • Isolation,
  • Being overlooked for training opportunities and promotion,
  • Being unable to work effectively if equipment was unreliable,
  • Feeling frustrated and de-motivated when things went wrong.

The risks identified by the managers were:

  • Individuals may not be suited to flexible working,
  • The investment in time and effort may be wasted,
  • Unreliable equipment could lead to ineffective working,
  • The team may become de-motivated and lose cohesion.

The expected benefits identified by the individuals and managers (as outlined in the previous section) were sufficient for them to accept this level of risk and go ahead with the flexible working scheme.

Consequences

The consequences of this decision were considered using data collected 18 months after the scheme began. The main result was that many managers had developed an even more positive attitude towards flexible working. They felt that it had been very successful, largely due to the positive attitude and commitment of the flexible workers and themselves at the outset. Managers felt that flexible workers were productive and well motivated due to less travelling and less complicated personal arrangements. Together they had avoided the problems of isolation and loss of team cohesion. Individuals felt that they were treated fairly and that their status had not been affected either positively or detrimentally by flexible working.

Monitoring Performance

The positive consequences of the scheme had modified the individuals' attitudes in the course of the succeeding 18 months. Participants, both managers and flexible workers, were keen to recommend flexible working to others.

The situation had evolved continually, highlighting the incremental nature of building trusting relationships. Since the scheme began the participants had endeavoured to deal with problems as they arose. For example, a whereabouts diary had been established on the Intranet to overcome difficulties of knowing when individuals would be available for meetings and when they would be contactable. The issue of contactability is important from two perspectives: the short term problem it creates and the long term effect that difficulties in contacting people may have on the overall relationship.

Some potential problems still existed. Managers were still concerned about the feasibility of managing someone of whom they had no previous knowledge. Other issues had become important more recently. Workers were concerned that they would lose the ability to work in this way if they changed jobs, as it may not be acceptable to another manager.

The implications for future work styles

Trust will be a key factor in future work arrangements. Individuals will become more autonomous and direct supervision will no longer feasible, due to such factors as physical separation or increasing skill specialisation. The level of autonomy shown by the individuals in this case is representative of how people may be working in the future.

The use of the trust model to analyse this flexible working study highlights the factors which proved to be important in supporting trust in this context. A working relationship based on trust enables both manager and worker to operate effectively regardless of where they are. The voluntary nature of the scheme ensured a positive commitment from all participants from the outset. This was later seen as a key element in its success. As this type of working extends further, there may be others who will not embrace such new arrangements so readily. These individuals may need support and coaching to help them become more independent and self-reliant.

This case also highlights how the role of the manager is changing. There is increasing emphasis on the ability to coach individuals and empower them to produce good quality work within the required timescales rather than supervise and monitor activity directly. Management styles, which are based on the close control of subordinates, are not appropriate for this type of working arrangement. The participants in the trial commented that their managers' styles, which were open and inclusive, were suited to this situation, but that a more interventionist, controlling style would not work. The importance to the individuals in this study, of being treated fairly, also emphasises that coaching and support is what they need from managers in order to build up trust from their point of view.

A paradox of modern organisations is that, while wanting the flexibility and creativity, which can be achieved through empowering individuals, organisations also want more centralised control over their activities. One way of achieving this is through cultural control. A strong corporate culture also keeps individuals, who are physically dispersed, effectively integrated within the work group and organisation as a whole. It is acknowledged that cultural change is very difficult to achieve and may take several years. Many large organisations have been going through this process over the last decade or so.

This case highlights the importance of previous experience and knowledge of other people's work as an important factor in a trusting relationship. It ensures confidence in the competence of the individual to do the work required and familiarity with the strengths and weaknesses of different members of a team. When individuals are physically dispersed it will be increasingly important to actively maintain networks of contacts, within and across organisational boundaries.

One of the key risks highlighted by both managers and individuals was the problem of unreliable equipment. This is an instance where there is not only a short-term impact on the productivity of the individual directly affected, but also a potential long-term impact on the trust relationship between the individual and their manager.

The stability reflected in the case study may not be typical of the future world of work. Individuals will be less likely to have just one job for several years at a time with one organisation. In the future, existing relationships and networks of contacts may not necessarily be within the same organisation. An unresolved concern of the managers in this case was how they would manage a flexible worker with whom they had no previous interaction. The problem of how to integrate new members into an existing work group without the advantage of physical proximity still needs to be addressed.
