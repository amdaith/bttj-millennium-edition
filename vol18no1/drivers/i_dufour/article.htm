Core Voice Telephony

Ian Dufour, Manager of Central Design Group
 

Voice and language have been the basis of face-to-face communication since the beginning of humanity, but remote communication began with drumbeats and smoke signals. Remote electrical communication also began with codes, known as telegraphy - the forerunner of a later communications revolution. However, it took the invention of the telephone in 1876 to bring in the era of remote communication using the human voice. This spawned a huge industry of telephone companies and equipment suppliers that led to the world-wide telephone system that we all know today.

For the last 124 years voice telephony has been the mainstay of the telecommunications system. It started with connections between users being established by people called operators. In small communities at least, they would recognise customers by their voice, know the location of customers when not at home and pass on messages. Throughout the last century, despite several generations of automatic switching systems from Strowger to digital, and the introduction of vast computer processing capabilities, we have been trying with only mixed success to emulate the capabilities of the manual operator.

Part of this emulation we call Intelligent Networks, or IN. With IN, value-added capability is provided through general-purpose computers used during call set-up for number-change services like freephone, premium-rate calls, chargecard and virtual private network services. In mobile networks a similar computing capability has been used to determine the location of the customer for call-delivery and for the billing of originated calls.

Other aspects of the operator's role that we are emulating result in voice-recognition systems and messaging systems both of which have only come into their own in the last decade. Speech-to-text and text-to-speech systems and language-translation systems have still to realise their full promise.

As the century changes, the dominance of voice is coming to an end. It will remain important but the growth of non-voice services, driven by the Internet and the client/server computer model, allied to the Web browser, will start to dominate communications traffic. Five years into the new century non-voice will represent many times the number of bits of voice traffic.

So what will happen to voice communication in this new era? Firstly, it will remain the most important person-to-person mode of communication, especially for those on the move. Secondly, the current investments in fixed and mobile networks, which are optimised for voice communication, will remain in place for many more years. And finally, we can predict a growing use of voice over data to make use of the infrastructure being put in place to support the massive growth in non-voice Internet-based services.

As the years go by, voice will get absorbed by the data revolution and become a minority service, but fortunately the basic human need for voice communication will remain as important as it has ever been.
