The Future of Research - Collaboration

Richard Nicol, Manager of Advanced Communications Research
 

Research in the GPO was against a background of not being able to obtain from manufacturers the dedicated switching and transmission equipment needed for network growth. The GPO provided basic research for the UK's telecommunications equipment manufacturers of the day. Our customer was very much UK plc.

With privatisation, research became the means by which BT obtained competitive edge through technology foresight of, in particular, the point technologies that have driven our business. As evidenced in this millennium edition, BT has led the world in demonstrating the feasibility of many new technologies including digital switching and fibre optics. BT Laboratories (now Adastral Park) have a patenting and publication record of which it can be proud.

During the 1970s and 1980s, much of BT Laboratories' work was devoted to achieving the highest bandwidth kilometre per pound. In the early 1970s the circular waveguide project was seen as an answer to the bandwidth challenge - a programme to feed microwaves through a national network of pipes. Ironically, we used laser beams to ensure that the waveguides were aligned - the feasibility of optical fibre transmission was proven; but, we had to wait for improvements in glass technology before fibres could deliver the high international bandwidths at cost levels that allowed the information superhighway to develop.

During the same period we also saw the rise of computing power. Gordon Moore's Law has proven to be an accurate predicter of technology trends for about 25 years. Today Moore's Law predictions are becoming understated.

Since the Internet entered everyone's agenda about five years ago (let's not forget that, as well as BT Laboratories, the Internet was 30 years old during 1999), we have seen an explosion in the power of technologies and applications. Processor power is doubling every six months; the 1 Gbit memory chip was recently announced; wavelength division multiplexing will increase wide-area network bandwidth by a factor of 64 in a couple of years, and by a factor of 200 in about five years and deliver the first Petabit networks; multimedia technology will increase by a factor of 100 in three years; storage will increase by a factor of seven in two years. These massive changes are blurring the boundary between computing and communications and changing the structure of both industries. The converged industry's massive growth is attracting more new suppliers every day. It also means that we are now spoilt for choice. As little as 10 years ago, BT would have to watch tens of suppliers world-wide; now it has to watch hundreds, and soon thousands, ranging in size from garage enterprises to major corporations. Often the products and services on offer are very similar or provide alternative means for doing the same thing. Previously the company had to decide how to have things made, it now has to decide not only whether to make or buy but also who to buy from and how to affect what they sell. The big issues for the business are rapidly moving from whether we can do something to what is the best way to do it.

The new environment - the end of end-to-end

There is a paradox to this convergence. The arrival of masses of niche players means that the industry is simultaneously fragmenting. Where once a national telco could control all aspects of delivering telecommunications services, each and every aspect of service delivery can now be unbundled. Global standardisation has meant that the proprietary solution is a thing of the past. BT, and every other telco, is now in an era where the speed of change, both technological and commercial, bears no resemblance to that experienced to date.

Deciding on the best way is the major challenge of the next decade. The global network is expanding at an unprecedented rate; applications and services are exploding with new products launched every day; the way people interact with technology is changing too. We need to develop greater understanding of interactions - between people, between people and machines, between machines (soon everything on the planet can have its own IP address), between protocols and hardware, and between different protocols. We are seeing a new economics for the profitable supply of global telecommunications services. The arrival of eBusiness is not just about automating what exists. The drive to re-engineer the corporation in the early 1980s was ahead of the technology being available, and did not achieve its full potential. Now we have the technology. As a result, business models are changing every day. For example, two years ago Encyclopedia Britannica declared that its product was too complex to move on to CDRom - now it's free on the Internet, and FreeServe changed the UK business model for Internet service provision.

The number of people connected to wireless networks world-wide will grow to over a billion by 2004; that year will also see data being the greatest volume of traffic on the UK PSTN, and the mobile network carrying 70% of voice traffic. In 2004 it is likely that English will cease to be the dominant language of the Internet. These predictions originated in the very first research campaigns that we set up over five years ago, and have provided the company with foresight, and helped change the business strategy to become a new wave data, mobility and multimedia company. The predictions were made in a relatively stable environment. The future promises less structure and more chaos - the importance of foresight cannot be underestimated.

No business can reasonably expect to cover all the ground itself. As in commerce, research will need to expand with BT's alliances and acquisitions. The new world will need new knowledge, new ideas and an even greater degree of innovation - all areas where research is really important. Innovation is not just ideas it is also about action. In this fast changing environment watching is not an option. The business must be able to adopt new technologies and the route into the company is to exploit the competencies of its research to relate technology to the business and the business to the technology. Research will need to increase its influence in the suppliers' world. An essential enabler is a strong reputation for good science applied to solving business problems.

As the head of BT's research community, my responsibilities are to make sure that all the research we do can be related to the business. We do not do research for the sake of science, but our science still needs to be world class. One challenge is to solve problems before they are apparent to the business.

Our research collaborations will increase - with suppliers, with universities (especially the world's best), with BT's venture partners, with customers, and the market-place. While researchers will still specialise to build depth, we will use our own technology to understand the broad picture as well. Commercial prudence must not stifle innovation. Recently I have been presented with a four-page collaboration agreement for working with a major telecomms lab and a seven-page, seven-year non-disclosure agreement just to see a Powerpoint presentation! There's clearly some cultural work to be done.

A high proportion of our work will be available for external exploitation. Researchers may find themselves not only working with technology start-up companies but also creating them. To understand the new complex, chaotic telecommunications environment we must ask some fairly naïve questions such as:

  • How does it relate to the existing business?
  • How will customers relate to it?
  • Is it a fundamental or temporary solution?
  • Where is the money?

We also have to use the best means of answering these questions. Our natural approach is to first use analysis and simulation techniques to gain insights. Theoretical models provide part of the answer; however, the days of real experiment are not over. In January 2000 my researchers will begin building AdastralNet - a testbed which will be used to explore technological options and the business options that result. AdastralNet will consist of both structured and unstructured networks for both fixed and mobile systems offering high and low bandwidths, as well as advanced products and services. As well as being used by BT, AdastralNet will form the core of the Adastral Research Network, and attract partners and collaborators by providing the broadest range of inter-linked technologies available anywhere. With our partners and collaborators we can then explore how the next generation of technology will be used to supply both commercial and domestic communications, and the value chains and business structures best suited to the eBusiness world.

We are in a technology-led business - technological advantage is one of our key differentiators; but, we must also make it easy for our customers to exploit this technological advantage and extend it to their business. In many instances all we have to do is to provide connectivity or bandwidth and the customers will find the ways of filling it themselves; in other situations we will have to lead the ideas for new services and applications.

Innovation is about understanding needs and identifying the best solution, whether new or existing, and finally taking action, and making it happen for BT and for the rest of the world. To do this effectively, we will need to combine the intellectual power gained from our in-house research with work from the wide range of collaborators within the Adastral Research Network, that is to exploit the technology leverage gained from being part of a large network of collaborating technology companies. So the future for research is to be not only the brain of the company but at the heart also.
