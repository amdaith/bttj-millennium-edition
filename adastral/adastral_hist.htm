Adastral Park - History

Adastral Park has a long history as a location for research and development, stretching back to 1914.

RAF Martlesham Heath: Martlesham Heath was virgin heathland until January 16th, 1917, when it became an airfield used by the RAF for aviation research. In 1941, the airfield became part of RAF Fighter Command. During World War II, many notable pilots flew from the airfield including Douglas Bader, who sent his famous telegram 'This squadron is off the air' while stationed at Martlesham Heath. Several buildings from the old airfield remain on the site, including the old Post Room which was the RAF camp hospital and mortuary and is now a listed building. The B2 building was the RAF armoury and is designed so that, in the event of an explosion inside the building, its specially built roof will blow off.

The Post Office Research Centre: The General Post Office (GPO) bought a 110 acre site on the Martlesham Heath airfield in 1968 to allow it to move out of its previous premises in Dollis Hill, London, and elsewhere. It was attracted to the site because the surrounding countryside is relatively flat - ideal for testing the radio-based communication systems in vogue at the time. The tower that's part of the main research building was specifically designed to enable this work. The Main Laboratory Block was the first new building to be built on the site. Incorporating the radio tower, it was designed to echo the lines of an upturned Saxon ship. (The Sutton Hoo burial site, at which a Saxon ship was found, is located nearby in Woodbridge.) The Queen officially opened the site in 1975 when the first researchers moved into the new building.

BT Laboratories: When the postal and telecommunications activities of the Post Office were separated, ownership of the site moved to British Telecommunications and its name was changed to BT Research Laboratories. Later, the name was shortened to BT Laboratories. Several more recent buildings were constructed to support BT's pioneering work on optical fibre technology and the fabrication of the highly-reliable transistors and integrated circuits required for submarine cable transmission systems. Among these, the B54 building, built to house clean rooms to make semiconductor devices, was the largest purpose built research clean room facility in Europe when it was built. It is nicknamed the Queen Mary because of its three red funnels. The latest building to be constructed on the site is the restaurant, which was opened in 1994. The building provides meeting rooms and feeds lunch to around 1500 customers each day.

Adastral Park: In June 1999, the site's name was changed to Adastral Park. This new name provides a link to the former use as a part of RAF Martlesham Heath and is derived from the Royal Air Force's motto - Per Ardua Ad Astra, or through adversity to the stars. The Park's logo is based on Ursa Minor (the Little Bear) - the constellation that includes the Stella Polaris (Pole Star) at the furthest point of its 'handle.' This star has been used for centuries by navigators to determine their location and guide their journey. Today, the people who work at Adastral Park help guide BT and its customers into the future.
